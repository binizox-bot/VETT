At Sandport Academy Oval in East Bay, this semi-final started with Coastvale A choosing to bat and then almost immediately giving the match a shape Harborline A spent the rest of the evening trying to undo, because Darian Holt and Elias Reed did not simply survive the new ball, they turned it into a long stretch of scoreboard pressure that left the fielding side chasing the game before the first wicket had even arrived. Elias was the cleaner early timer, driving anything full and lifting the shorter ball when Harborline missed their length, while Darian played the heavier innings beside him, a little more watchful at the start and then gradually more severe once he had worked out which bowlers could be attacked. Their opening stand of one hundred and twenty-three was not a loose, chaotic dash either. It had rhythm. It had control. It had the sort of repeated boundary pressure that makes a captain start shifting fields before the bowler has really lost the over. Harborline tried several angles, but Cole Bennett and Jude Foster leaked badly in the original power phase of their spell equivalents, and in this reconstructed card Theo Grant and Riley Ford never quite created the squeeze that would have made the openers rethink. Jasper Wynn eventually removed Elias for fifty-eight, and that wicket did matter because it broke the first major axis of the innings, but it came after Coastvale had already created the kind of base from which a two-hundred score was not only possible but expected. Darian stayed, and that was the important part. Luca Shore entered with the right intent and turned the next phase into another sharp push rather than a pause. His thirty from sixteen gave Coastvale the second burst they needed after the opening stand, and the partnership with Darian moved the score from the low one-twenties to one hundred and sixty-seven before Miles Carter finally cut him off. That was Harborline’s best window with the ball, because Milo Carter and Kai Brooks went almost immediately after, and for a few deliveries Coastvale looked as though they might squander the base in exactly the way strong sides sometimes do when the platform becomes almost too comfortable. But Darian had already done too much damage and, even after he fell for seventy-eight at one hundred and eighty-six, Owen Vale and Nate Cross squeezed enough from the close to carry Coastvale to one hundred and ninety-eight for five. The bowling figures explain the first innings better than a simple total does. Miles Carter’s two wickets and his economy were valuable, Finn-Harper-style control in a high-scoring environment, while Jasper Wynn, Nolan Pierce and Arlo Dean all found moments that stopped the final number from becoming even heavier. But Harborline never erased the opening damage. They were always correcting after the fact, always searching for the wicket that had needed to arrive six overs earlier. Chasing one hundred and ninety-nine, Harborline A began with the kind of urgency the target demanded, Tyler Vale and Evan Marsh taking twenty-six from the first little phase and making it clear that they would not crawl into the chase. Caleb Stone, though, gave Coastvale the first real defensive control point. He removed Evan Marsh and then Tyler as well, and those wickets were not just names on the scorecard; they changed Harborline from a side attacking a big target into a side needing to rebuild while still keeping pace with it. Liam Brooks briefly helped the innings along but fell to Dylan Shaw, and although Nolan Price and Julian Frost restored some order, Julian’s run-out at eighty-five for four was one of those understated moments that cut a chase in half before people fully notice it. Nolan then went at one hundred and five, and at that stage Harborline were not out of the game, but they were running out of clean structures. That is why Blake Ward’s innings mattered so much. His fifty-nine from thirty-six was the hand that made the chase feel alive again, and he batted with the correct kind of controlled aggression: enough fours to keep the field moving, enough power to make the target look reachable, and enough composure to build with Isaac Moore rather than simply swing himself out of the match. Isaac’s twenty-three from thirteen was exactly the support Blake needed. Their stand of fifty-nine changed the atmosphere of the game, because from one hundred and five for five Harborline suddenly moved to one hundred and sixty-four for six, and for the first time since the opening overs Coastvale had to think about the possibility that their huge platform might still be hunted down. Then came the decisive defensive moment. Isaac was run out with just enough time left for the chase to remain mathematically alive but no longer structurally balanced, and once that partnership was broken, Blake had too much of the finish to carry alone. Cole Bennett stayed with him briefly, and Blake kept finding scoring options, but Dylan Shaw removed him at one hundred and seventy-three, and that dismissal ended the real contest. Aaron Quinn was bowled soon after, Finn Harper and Cole Bennett completed the innings, and Harborline closed on one hundred and seventy-six for eight. That final score looks respectable because it is respectable, but it also tells the deeper story of the chase: Harborline had bursts, they had one very dangerous counterstand, and they had Blake Ward’s excellent resistance, yet they never matched the clean continuity Coastvale had found at the top. Coastvale’s defense was not flawless. Logan Frost and Ryan Cole were expensive, and Harborline were allowed enough scoring phases to make the last third interesting. Still, Caleb Stone’s early double strike, Dylan Shaw’s two key wickets, Noah Blake’s removal of Nolan Price, Lucas Kane’s late wicket and the two run-outs gave Coastvale exactly the interruptions a side defending one hundred and ninety-eight needs. The match therefore resolves around two connected truths. First, Darian Holt and Elias Reed won Coastvale the right to dictate the chase before Harborline had even batted. Second, Harborline’s best recovery through Blake Ward and Isaac Moore arrived from too far behind and was broken just before it could turn into full control. Darian Holt was the correct player of the match because his seventy-eight did not merely top-score; it gave the innings its spine, turned the first half into Coastvale’s property, and made every later Harborline mistake more expensive. Harborline fought hard enough to keep the scoreline honest, especially through Blake Ward, but Coastvale owned the largest partnership, the stronger platform, and the better-timed defensive breaks. That is why the twenty-two-run result feels earned rather than inflated.
