At Greenfield Cricket Ground in Eastport, Ridgecrest won the toss, chose to bat, and built the kind of first innings that did not come from one single uncontrollable explosion but from repeated, disciplined construction, one phase replacing another just when Shoreline thought they had found a way back into the game. Archer Vale and Brandon Cole began carefully rather than recklessly, and that mattered because Shoreline’s new-ball bowlers were not giving them a free start. Brandon was the first to settle into a little rhythm, finding a couple of early scoring options before Julian Brooks straightened one enough to bowl him, while Archer played the slower, more watchful role and helped keep the opening damage from becoming immediate. The first stand had not been dominant, but it had done enough to prevent Shoreline from owning the opening spell. Cameron Reed then walked in and changed the tone of the innings. He was the first Ridgecrest batter who looked as though he could turn ordinary overs into expensive ones, strong through the off side and quick enough to punish anything overpitched. Archer stayed with him for a short second phase before Hudson Lane had him caught, but by then Cameron had already given the innings a stronger pulse. The real early-middle acceleration came when Dominic Cross joined Cameron. Dominic’s 31 from 30 was exactly the sort of innings that keeps a fifty-over batting card from falling into a holding pattern, because he did not merely survive beside the set player; he drove, rotated, and kept Shoreline from building a single plan around Cameron. Their partnership moved Ridgecrest from a still-manageable position into the first genuinely useful platform of the day, and although Evan Marsh broke it with a caught-and-bowled, the innings had already passed its first stress test. Elliot Hale gave a short burst before Gavin Price pinned him, and that wicket briefly gave Shoreline a chance to squeeze, especially with the score not yet at the point where the final ten overs could be played entirely on instinct. But Ridgecrest found the next layer through Finley Ward and Cameron Reed. Cameron’s 76 from 72 was the central batting hand in the first half, not quite a hundred-maker’s domination, but a high-value innings because it joined the restart after the second wicket to the stronger middle stretch. Finley gave the card a second controlling voice, and once Cameron finally fell to Logan West, Ridgecrest were no longer just building; they were setting up a score that could stretch Shoreline in a chase. The most important late partnership came between Finley and Gavin Moss. Finley’s 48 from 44 had tempo without needless risk, while Gavin’s 39 from 41 kept the scoreboard turning through a phase where the field was spread and the bowlers were trying to buy time with defensive lengths. Their stand pushed Ridgecrest into the final overs with the sort of base that lets a lower order swing selectively rather than desperately. Julian Brooks removed Finley, Dylan Archer’s run-out ended Gavin’s stay, and Logan West cut out Oliver Kent, but Ridgecrest still kept finding small late pieces. Isaac Rowe’s 18 from 14 gave the innings a useful shove, Jordan Pike’s short stay helped move it toward the last over, and Kyle Rowan’s unbeaten nine from six balls, including a late strike over the rope, gave the final total a little extra sting. Ridgecrest were all out on the last ball for 307, and while Shoreline had taken wickets at regular enough intervals to avoid a complete runaway, they had never stopped the innings long enough to make one wicket feel like the beginning of a collapse. Shoreline’s bowling had respectable individual pieces: Gavin Price’s ten overs were the most controlled, Logan West took the important wicket of Cameron and later Oliver, Julian Brooks claimed two useful breaks, Hudson Lane picked up two wickets even while leaking runs, and Evan Marsh removed Dominic just as that stand was beginning to look dangerous. Still, the broader problem was that every Ridgecrest wicket was followed by another usable batting phase. Chasing 308, Shoreline were immediately forced into repair mode. Blake Turner fell in the first over to Oliver Kent, Carter Holt was pinned by Kyle Rowan soon after, and Dylan Archer’s lively little counter was also cut short by Rowan. At three down early, the chase was close to being over before it had even acquired shape. That is why the fourth-wicket partnership between Aaron Vale and Evan Marsh became the emotional center of the second innings. Aaron was the more fluent and more aggressive of the two, playing with real authority through the covers and square of the wicket, while Evan took the longer view, absorbing the pressure that comes with chasing a large target after an early collapse. Their stand did not begin as a charge. It began as rescue. Then it became structure. Then, for a while, it became genuine pressure on Ridgecrest. Aaron’s 86 from 83 had ten fours and two sixes, and every time the chase seemed ready to slow into resignation he found a boundary or a hard-run pair that kept the target just inside imagination. Evan’s innings was quieter but nearly as important, because his 84 from 102 gave Shoreline continuity through several phases of the chase. Together they dragged the score past the point where Ridgecrest could simply wait for mistakes. Jordan Pike eventually produced the wicket that changed the chase most sharply, having Aaron stumped after the partnership had moved Shoreline into serious territory. Felix Grant then arrived and struck brightly, his 21 from 16 briefly keeping the chase alive with hard boundaries rather than soft survival, but Jordan removed him too before that cameo could become the next major stand. Evan remained, and with Gavin Price he built another important passage. Gavin’s 16 from 28 was not explosive, yet it served its purpose by keeping Evan company and preventing the collapse from restarting too soon. That stand carried Shoreline deep enough that the match still demanded Ridgecrest’s best closing work. Gavin Moss then took over at a crucial moment, bowling Gavin Price and later removing Evan himself, and that second wicket effectively ended Shoreline’s serious chance. Before Evan fell, however, Kyle Rowan had already re-entered the match as the decisive finisher. He removed Hudson Lane, then Ian Foster, and after Evan was bowled by Gavin Moss, Rowan came back to end Julian Brooks’ resistance and complete a five-wicket spell. The quality of Rowan’s performance was in its timing as much as its figures. He had struck early when Shoreline were trying to recover from the first wicket, then came back late when the chase still had enough life through Evan to become uncomfortable. His five wickets for 40 were not cosmetic; they were distributed exactly across the moments Ridgecrest needed control. Shoreline were eventually bowled out for 253 in the forty-seventh over, a total that showed how hard Aaron and Evan had fought but also how little support remained once the two main innings were broken. The match therefore belonged to Ridgecrest because their batting was more layered and their bowling found the right cut points. Cameron Reed shaped the middle of the first innings, Finley Ward and Gavin Moss stretched it, and the lower order kept the final overs productive. Shoreline had one outstanding recovery and one smaller late push, but they never built the same chain of contributions. Aaron Vale and Evan Marsh made the chase real; Kyle Rowan made sure it never became complete. That is why his player-of-the-match award fits: a small finishing hand with the bat, then five wickets placed at the start and end of the chase, exactly where Ridgecrest needed the game won.
