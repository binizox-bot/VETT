Good morning from Central Academy Oval in Shorehaven, where Coastport won the toss, put Highland Vale in, and then controlled the game in the most useful way: not by blowing the first innings apart in one dramatic spell, but by refusing to let Highland Vale turn any promising stretch into a full platform, and then by chasing with enough calm that the early wickets never became a real route back into the match. Highland Vale’s innings began with a little movement and a little illusion of comfort, mostly because Isaac Warner came out busier than anyone else in the top order and because Coastport were not completely clean with the ball in the opening exchanges. Isaac’s eighteen from sixteen gave the first few overs some life, but the stand itself was already telling a more complicated story, with several free runs helping the board along while Ellis Ward was still feeling his way into the day. Liam Turner made the first useful incision when he bowled Isaac, and that wicket mattered because it stopped Highland Vale from turning a bright, slightly untidy start into something that could pressure the fielding side early. Ellis Ward and Julian Shore then gave the innings its first proper shape. Julian was not fluent, and that is important to say plainly, because his fourteen came from a long enough stay to show he was doing more holding than driving, while Ellis became the batter asked to carry the innings through its first serious rebuild. Their stand reached forty-five, and although a few extras again helped it, the partnership did at least carry Highland Vale from the danger of a single early wicket into a position where they could imagine batting deep. Connor Blake ended that thought by having Julian caught by Lucas Grant, and that was the first sign of the pattern Coastport would repeat all afternoon: let a partnership begin, let it look useful, then cut it before it becomes the innings. Ellis Ward was still there, and for a short passage with Tyler Brook he looked like the one batter Highland Vale might build around, but Caleb Shaw’s wicket of Ellis for thirty-eight changed the innings sharply. It was not simply the removal of an opener; it was the end of the only top-order player who had combined time with some scoring control. Tyler Brook and Nathan Vale then produced the most important middle-order stand, forty-two for the fourth wicket, steady rather than forceful, with Tyler making twenty-four and Nathan twenty-eight, but Connor Blake came back at precisely the right time. He removed Tyler, and then, after Noah Foster had pinned Blake Rowan first ball, Connor took Nathan as well, which turned a workable 133 for four into a much more fragile 145 for six. That was the first-innings hinge. Highland Vale had not collapsed completely, but their chance of pushing toward something genuinely difficult had been badly reduced. Cole Hart then played the late hand that kept the innings from fading into the low one-sixties. His twenty-two was not expansive, but it was practical, especially with Logan Pierce staying around near the end. Aaron Dale and Jude Moss could not help for long, Aiden Brooks removing both, and the ninth-wicket partnership between Cole and Logan was the last bit of resistance before Liam Turner returned to finish the innings. Highland Vale were all out for 181 in the final over of the innings, and the total felt neither disastrous nor truly secure. The batting card explains why. Ellis Ward gave the innings its early spine, Nathan Vale and Tyler Brook added some middle-order substance, Cole Hart dragged the tail forward, and Isaac Warner gave the start some pace, but nobody reached fifty, nobody owned the innings, and too many partnerships ended just before the next stage could begin. Coastport’s bowling was broad and well-timed. Liam Turner’s three wickets framed the innings, Connor Blake’s three through the middle were the most structurally damaging, Aiden Brooks cut through the late order, Caleb Shaw removed Ellis at a critical point, Noah Foster broke the immediate recovery attempt, and Owen Parker’s wicketless spell gave control at the other end. That mix left Highland Vale with a total they could defend only if the new ball brought real pressure. It almost did, briefly. Ethan Cole began Coastport’s reply with crisp intent, three boundaries in his sixteen, and the first stand moved briskly enough to keep the chase ahead of anxiety, but Finn Carter removed him with Cole Hart holding the catch, and then Logan Pierce got Mason Reed soon after. At thirty-eight for two, Highland Vale had at least made the pursuit feel less automatic. Connor Blake, however, was already settling into the innings that would decide the match. What made his unbeaten seventy-four so valuable was not just the volume of runs, but the way he adjusted to each partner and each phase. With Ethan, he could watch the ball being hit around him. With Mason, he simply had to keep the innings from wobbling further. With Lucas Grant, he rebuilt properly. Lucas’s twenty-four from fifty-one was slow, yet it was exactly the stabilising hand the chase needed after two early wickets. Their stand of fifty did not crush Highland Vale in spectacle, but it drained their best chance of creating pressure. By the time Jude Moss had Lucas caught behind, Coastport had reached eighty-eight, Connor was set, and the match was already beginning to tilt away from the bowling side. Noah Foster then played the finishing hand with a clarity that made the last part of the chase feel almost clinical. His unbeaten forty-six from forty-seven was positive without being reckless, and that balance was important because Connor did not need a partner who merely survived. He needed someone who would stop Highland Vale from bowling dry overs at one set batter. Noah gave him exactly that, finding the gaps, taking the boundary when the length allowed, and making sure the target kept shrinking even when Connor was working in singles. Their unbroken stand of ninety-four was the decisive batting passage of the match. Highland Vale had taken three wickets by that stage, yet they never got the fourth, and without that fourth wicket the chase simply lost its tension. Finn Carter, Jude Moss and Logan Pierce all had moments, but the attack as a whole could not keep the ball tight enough after the twentieth over, and the extras in the chase, while not extreme, still helped Coastport avoid the kind of dry spell that might have made things awkward. Connor Blake’s player-of-the-match case was therefore very strong across both innings: three wickets when Highland Vale were trying to build, then seventy-four not out when Coastport needed a controlled chase. Noah Foster’s unbeaten support was crucial, Liam Turner’s three wickets mattered, and several Coastport bowlers played their part, but Connor was the player who appeared at both decisive centers of the game. Highland Vale were competitive in pieces, yet Coastport were better at converting those pieces into a full match: they broke the first innings at the right times, then recovered from an early chase wobble through a long, calm, unbroken finish. That is why the seven-wicket margin looked comfortable by the end, because Coastport had not merely chased the total; they had slowly removed every way Highland Vale might have defended it.
