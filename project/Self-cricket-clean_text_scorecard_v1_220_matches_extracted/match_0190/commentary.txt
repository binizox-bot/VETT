At Lakeside Grammar Oval in Moorhaven, Westmere won the toss and chose to bat, and for the first dozen overs the innings looked as though it might become one of those low, awkward cards where the top order never quite gives the middle any room to breathe. Kai Mercer struck a couple of early boundaries and gave the score a little motion, but Noah Vale removed him before the first stand could become a platform. Tomas Reed stayed longer without ever looking fluent, Arun Hale came and went cheaply, and by the time Westmere reached thirty-four for three, the innings had already become a repair job rather than a normal first-innings build. Nolan Frost then changed the shape of the match. His seventy-nine was the only innings with real authority on the Westmere card, and the way he built it mattered because it was not just a rescue, it was a counterweight against the early damage. He drove firmly when Riverton overpitched, lifted the tempo whenever the field crept in, and used his boundary options well enough to make the bowlers stop attacking in straight lines. Ben Ellis played the opposite hand and did so with a kind of stubborn usefulness that can be easy to underrate. His forty-seven from one hundred and thirteen balls was slow, sometimes almost painfully so, but it gave Nolan the stable partner the innings desperately needed after the early damage. Their fourth-wicket stand of one hundred and sixteen carried Westmere from thirty-four for three all the way to one hundred and fifty for four, and for a long spell it looked as though Westmere might turn a broken start into something genuinely defendable. The partnership also explains the odd texture of the innings: one batter was driving the score, the other was protecting the structure, and together they made Riverton work through a long middle phase without the easy wicket they had earned earlier. Caleb Ross broke the stand by removing Nolan, and that wicket changed everything. Once the captain was gone, Westmere lost the clean line of the innings almost immediately. Evan Rowe briefly found a boundary but Liam Shaw removed him, Milo Grant followed without scoring, Dylan Shore could not stay long, and when Ben Ellis finally fell at one hundred and sixty-six, the innings had already lost the chance to finish properly. Hugo Lane was bowled without adding anything, Vikram Holt was the final wicket, and Westmere closed on one hundred and seventy-three in the forty-seventh over. The collapse from one hundred and fifty for four to all out was the central regret of their innings. A score nearer two hundred had been available while Nolan and Ben were together, but once that stand ended the final six wickets added only twenty-three. Riverton’s bowling card reflects that two-part story. Noah Vale and Arlo Bennett helped create the early pressure, Liam Shaw kept damaging the middle and lower order, and Caleb Ross became the defining bowler with five for thirty-two, first cutting down Nolan and then sweeping through the late wickets that stopped Westmere from using their platform. Chasing one hundred and seventy-four, Riverton did not get a smooth passage. Rohan Mehta started brightly and Mason Cole gave him a sharp opening push, but Arun Hale removed Rohan at twenty-four and Nolan Frost struck immediately after, taking Mason caught and bowled with only one more run added. At twenty-five for two, the chase was open enough for Westmere to believe. Arlo Bennett walked in there and became the player the whole innings revolved around. His first job was not to win the game quickly; it was to stop the chase from becoming a panic. Finn Archer helped him rebuild in a practical, low-risk stand, and although Finn’s fourteen was modest, it let Arlo settle and begin choosing his moments. Ben Ellis eventually removed Finn at sixty-three, and again Westmere had a route back, but Arlo answered with the most controlled phase of the chase, adding fifty with Ethan Price. Ethan’s eleven was small, yet his stay mattered because it allowed Arlo to move from repair into command. Arlo struck cleanly through the middle overs, and when the bowling became too short or too full, he punished it with the kind of six-hitting that changed the arithmetic faster than Westmere could reset the field. Vikram Holt then gave Westmere their sharpest moment of hope, bowling Ethan and Noah Vale in successive balls at one hundred and sixteen. Suddenly Riverton were five down, and a chase that had looked under control was alive again. Caleb Ross stayed briefly, but Nolan Frost removed him at one hundred and thirty-one. Liam Shaw followed at one hundred and forty-three, lbw to Kai Mercer, and now the match had reached its true pressure point: Riverton still needed thirty-one, had only three wickets left, and the entire chase depended on Arlo refusing to blink. Owen Pike gave him exactly the support required. His nine not out from twenty was not a headline innings, but it was the correct innings for that moment: leave, block, steal the safe single, keep the set batter facing enough. Arlo did the heavier work, moving through the final stand with a mix of restraint and clean striking, finishing unbeaten on ninety-five from ninety-five balls. The final margin of three wickets captures the tension better than the target alone does, because Riverton were never allowed to coast; they kept being dragged back into awkward positions, and each time Arlo had to rebuild the chase’s shape. Westmere’s bowlers deserved credit for making it difficult. Nolan Frost backed up his batting with two wickets, Vikram Holt’s double strike nearly turned the match, and Arun Hale, Ben Ellis, and Kai Mercer all found important breakthroughs. But they could not remove the one player who mattered most. That is why Arlo Bennett was the clear Player of the Match. He contributed with the ball in the early squeeze, then carried the chase through every restart, every wobble, and every late pressure point. Westmere had one great stand and then a collapse. Riverton had one batter who stayed through the whole chase, and that was enough to turn a dangerous pursuit into a three-wicket win.
