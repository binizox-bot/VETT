At Summit Academy Ground in Westbridge, Rivermark chose to field first and spent most of the opening innings doing the one thing that matters most against a side trying to build a fifty-over score: they kept cutting off Highridge just when a partnership was starting to look useful, never quite allowing one batter to move from a start into full control, never letting the innings breathe long enough for the scoreboard to turn from workable into genuinely threatening. Highridge did not collapse in one dramatic burst, and that is important, because this was not a card full of empty lines and panic. It was more frustrating than that from their point of view. Almost every section had a small contribution, almost every pair found a few overs of stability, and yet each time the batting side looked ready to make the next step, Rivermark found the wicket that pushed them back into another restart. Adrian Vale gave the innings its first clean scoring hand, making thirty-one with four boundaries and giving the opening stand with Evan Marsh enough early weight to get Highridge moving. Evan played the slower role, not entirely fluent but useful enough to hold one end while Adrian attacked the loose ball, and when Tyler Brooks bowled Adrian with the score on forty, the first platform had been formed without being allowed to grow. That became the whole pattern of the innings. Evan and Jordan Hayes then added another meaningful stand, Jordan striking more cleanly and briefly suggesting that Highridge might carry the early score into a stronger top-order foundation, but Ryan Cross removed Evan and then Jordan soon after, and suddenly a pair of thirty-plus stands had become no more than a decent beginning. Nathan Vale and Logan Pierce did the next repair job. Their partnership was not flashy and carried no obvious aggression, but it was important because it stopped Highridge from sliding after the top-order wickets. Nathan’s twenty-one was a holding innings, Logan’s twenty-six the slightly more active support, and together they took the score into the low one-twenties before Connor Hayes stepped in and shifted the innings again. First Nathan went, then Carter Miles followed immediately without scoring, and that double interruption changed the feel of the middle overs from careful construction into uneasy survival. Logan and Aiden Ford rebuilt a little, Aiden then joined Blake Rowan for another useful passage, and Blake himself became the late-innings anchor, but none of those stands ever quite escaped the same ceiling. Dylan Price removed Aiden before the seventh-wicket stand could become substantial. Tyler Brooks came back to stop Blake’s longer stay. Lucas Grant bowled Parker Lane after his brisk nineteen had finally given Highridge a bit of late pace. Sean Carter’s final flick and Cole Bennett’s not-out nudge carried the total past two hundred, but even the finish said a lot about the whole innings: useful, competitive, never dominant. Highridge closed on two hundred and six for nine, with the batting runs spread across Adrian, Jordan, Logan, Blake, Nathan, Aiden and Parker rather than gathered around one commanding figure. Rivermark’s bowling was therefore effective less because it produced a wild collapse and more because it prevented consolidation. Connor Hayes’ three wickets were the middle-overs spine of the effort. Tyler Brooks struck at the front and near the back. Ryan Cross removed two of the top three. Dylan Price’s controlled spell kept the scoring rate pinned. Lucas Grant gave away a few more but collected the important late wicket of Parker. Even the short overs from Liam Parker and Owen Hayes fit into a broader fielding plan where Highridge never found a long soft period. Chasing two hundred and seven, Rivermark had the kind of start that can make a medium target awkward, because Noah Blake fell almost immediately, caught behind off Sean Carter, and at three for one Highridge had the early wicket they needed if they were going to defend a score built more on accumulation than force. Ethan Cole, though, settled the whole chase almost from that moment. His first major stand with Liam Parker was the passage that took the danger out of the new ball. Liam made thirty-five with a couple of boundaries and a six, playing positively enough to stop Highridge from crowding Ethan with pressure, while Ethan moved from the early twenties into control without making the chase feel rushed. That partnership of eighty was the first clear dividing line in the second innings. Highridge had an opening, Rivermark shut it. Blake Rowan finally removed Liam, and for a short while the chase slowed into a more patient shape through Owen Hayes, whose seventeen from a long stay was not glamorous but was exactly the kind of innings that protects a set batter from being isolated. Ethan used that phase intelligently. He did not force the rate just because he was set. He kept the chase in front of the required pace, absorbed the tighter overs from Blake and Jordan Hayes, and waited for the next moment when acceleration would carry more value than risk. Carter Miles eventually removed Owen, but at one hundred and thirty-eight for three, Rivermark were already close enough that Highridge needed not one wicket but a cluster. Mason Reed prevented that cluster from forming. He came in with the game still technically open and played the sharpest support hand of the chase, twenty-six from twenty-three balls, rotating early, then finding the boundary when Highridge tried to force dots at one end. The fourth-wicket stand with Ethan was worth sixty-one and effectively settled the match. Ethan’s own scoring inside that phase became more assertive, moving through the hundred and then keeping the chase from tightening after Mason eventually fell at one hundred and ninety-nine. That wicket gave Sean Carter a second success, but by then the result was almost fully decided. Caleb Foster only had to stay with Ethan for the final few overs, and Ethan finished the job unbeaten on one hundred and eighteen from one hundred and twenty-two balls, with eleven fours and two sixes, the kind of chase innings that looks controlled because it was built in the right order: absorb the early wicket, build with Liam, steady with Owen, accelerate with Mason, finish without handing the game back. Highridge’s bowling had moments. Sean Carter took the first and fourth wickets. Blake Rowan was the most economical and removed Liam when the chase was beginning to flow. Carter Miles broke the third stand. Jordan Hayes and Cole Bennett offered periods of control. The problem was that nobody could remove Ethan Cole, and once that became clear, every Highridge wicket began to feel like an interruption rather than a genuine turn. Rivermark reached two hundred and eight for four in the forty-first over, and the six-wicket margin captures the game well. Highridge had enough batting to make a contest, but not enough dominance to put the chase under heavy scoreboard pressure. Rivermark had one batter who understood the chase completely and enough support around him to make sure his innings did not become stranded. That is why Ethan Cole was the obvious player of the match: not simply because he made the biggest score, but because he gave Rivermark a single continuous line through a chase that could otherwise have become awkward if Highridge had found two quick wickets at any of the restart points.
