At Meadowbank Oval in Eastbridge, Ridgeport won the toss, batted first, and slowly turned what began as a lively but manageable innings into the kind of one-sided afternoon that Coral Bay could never really pull back into shape. Milo Grant gave Ridgeport the early spark, not a long innings, not a dominating one, but enough clean contact to make the new ball feel less threatening, and when Caleb Stone finally had him caught by Toby Lane, Coral Bay had done something useful without yet doing anything decisive. The wicket brought Leo Marsh to Nathan Wells, and that was where the match began to tilt properly. Nathan had already looked settled, playing with enough timing to keep the scoreboard moving without forcing every stroke, and Leo came in with the right tempo, busy without being reckless, strong enough through the gaps to stop the field from closing around Nathan, and calm enough to make the partnership feel controlled rather than frantic. Coral Bay’s problem was that they could not build pressure from both ends. One over would contain a couple of good balls, then the next would leak a wide or a missed length, and Ridgeport kept getting little releases that meant the batters never had to manufacture everything themselves. Nathan took full advantage of that looseness. He did not sprint from the first ball; he built the innings in layers, first through placement, then through boundaries when the bowlers drifted, and later with the harder shots once the field had already begun to spread. Leo’s role mattered because he gave the stand a second scoring line. He was not just standing there while Nathan made the innings; he was rotating, punishing width, and making Coral Bay bowl to two set players instead of one. By the time Jonah Price finally removed Leo, caught by Ethan Parker, the second-wicket stand had done the real damage. Ridgeport had moved far beyond a platform and into control, and Coral Bay were already chasing the feeling of a breakthrough rather than the actual shape of the innings. Connor Blake then came in and kept the late section sharp. Nathan was still the main story, still finding enough power and timing to push the innings toward a score Coral Bay would hate chasing, but Connor gave him the proper support for the closing phase, hitting cleanly enough to make the final overs feel expensive even when Coral Bay did manage dots. Ryan Hale eventually bowled Nathan, and that at least denied Ridgeport the full unbeaten finish, but the damage had already been laid down: a hundred-plus innings, a huge central stand, and a scoreboard that had crossed into severe territory. Evan Brooks came and went quickly, Noah Finn getting something back for Coral Bay, yet Connor stayed there and Ben Carter helped him finish the last few balls without letting the innings sag. Ridgeport closed on two hundred and fifty-one for four from their thirty-one overs, and the total had several layers to it: Milo’s opening shove, Nathan Wells’ match-defining hundred, Leo Marsh’s long and intelligent support innings, Connor’s late punch, and a heavy extras column that Coral Bay will not enjoy reviewing because it kept turning pressure into free movement. The chase, in truth, never felt like a chase for long. Coral Bay needed not just a fast start, they needed authority, and instead the first few overs were mostly about survival mixed with extras. Toby Lane and Caleb Stone spent time in the middle, but the ball was not coming cleanly off the bat, and Ridgeport could afford to watch the required rate disappear into the distance because the batters were not really hurting them. Caleb’s long stay ended with Lucas Barrett bowling him, and although the score had moved into double figures by then, too much of that movement had come from Ridgeport’s looseness rather than Coral Bay’s control. Ben Carter then tightened the innings in a way that mattered more than simple figures. He gave Ridgeport the hard overs, the captain’s overs, the ones that said Coral Bay would not be allowed to breathe just because the target was already huge. Toby was caught behind by Leo Marsh, Owen Reed briefly gave the scorecard its only real batting pulse with a boundary and a few steadier moments, but Carter had him caught by Isaac Blake as well, and once Owen was gone the chase had no set batter left to build around. Noah Finn and Aiden Cole tried to drag the innings forward, but it was a crawl, not a recovery. Aiden stayed for a long time, and that has some value in a collapse, yet he was never able to turn time into pressure on the fielding side. Then Dylan Cole entered the middle overs and changed the innings from slow defeat into full disintegration. Noah was bowled, Ryan Hale followed almost immediately, and Liam Turner was gone before Coral Bay could even reset the mood. It was not quite the clean theatrical burst of three wickets in three balls, but it had the same practical effect on the contest: the middle order had been cut out before it could form a second line of resistance. Mason Reed tried to hang around with Aiden, and for a few deliveries Coral Bay at least avoided losing another wicket, but the running pressure told its own story. Aiden was run out by Leo Marsh after a long, cramped innings, Ethan Parker was pinned by Dylan Cole without scoring, and Coral Bay were reduced to tail-end survival with almost no scoring threat left. Mason was then run out with Dylan involved again, Jonah Price stayed unbeaten through the end, and Zach Perry resisted long enough to make Ridgeport bowl the innings out rather than have it vanish instantly, but Dylan eventually finished him as well. That final wicket completed a five-wicket spell that fit the tone of the second innings perfectly: not just wicket-taking, but suffocation. Ben Carter had already made the top order uncomfortable, Lucas Barrett had made the first incision, Evan Brooks and Daniel Cross kept the scoring narrow enough to prevent any release, and Dylan Cole turned the second half of the chase into a procession. Coral Bay’s batting runs barely got going, and the extras were almost as visible as the players with the bat, which tells you how little real control the chase ever found. Ridgeport did not merely win because Nathan Wells made a hundred, although that was the foundation and the cleanest reason he deserved Player of the Match. They won because the innings had structure from the second wicket onward, because Leo Marsh made sure the dominant phase did not depend on one batter alone, because Connor Blake gave the finish some bite, and because the bowlers treated a massive target as something to defend properly rather than something to admire. Coral Bay will look at this as a match that got away in layers: too many extras with the ball, too much time without a wicket during the central stand, too little intent at the start of the chase, and then too many dismissals once Dylan Cole found his rhythm. Ridgeport, by contrast, built the day cleanly: a strong platform, a huge anchor innings, a sharp finish, early squeeze with the ball, and a ruthless collapse through the middle and lower order. The margin was enormous, and it matched the cricket. This was not a close game hidden by late damage; it was a match Ridgeport seized through Nathan Wells’ bat and then finished through collective bowling discipline.
