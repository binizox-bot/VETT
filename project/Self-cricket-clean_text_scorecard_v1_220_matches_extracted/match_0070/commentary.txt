Good afternoon from Harbourview Oval in Northport, where Kingsport won the toss, chose to field, and then gave Westhaven just enough time in the middle to make the innings look like a contest before taking the game away in the two places that mattered most: first by never letting Westhaven turn repair into command, and then by making the D/L chase feel almost insultingly simple once Isaac Rowan and Luke Bennett got going. Westhaven’s start had the wrong sort of movement from the very beginning. There were runs on the board early, yes, but too much of that movement came through extras and not enough through the batters actually dictating the terms. Rowan Clarke was trapped in front by Nathan Cole after a scratchy opening stay, Ben Lawson followed when Connor Shaw found enough shape to bring Luke Bennett into the catch, and at fourteen for two the batting side had already lost the clean start they would have wanted after being sent in. Callum Reed, the captain, was the first player to make the innings feel alive rather than merely defensive. He did not last deep enough to own the day, but his thirty-one had proper presence in it, a couple of firm boundaries and two clean strikes over the rope, and with Daniel Moss he gave Westhaven their first meaningful stand. Daniel’s role was very different. He was not trying to rush the game forward. He was absorbing, leaving, working into space, and making sure the card did not keep breaking every few overs. Together they added the largest stand of Westhaven’s innings, and for that short period the batting side looked as if it might drag itself toward a useful full-length total. Mason Hart ended that by removing Callum, and the moment mattered because it cut away the only aggressive top-order hand before Daniel’s occupation could become something stronger. Elliot Shaw then came in and played a long supporting role beside Daniel, not fluent, not especially forceful, but valuable enough because it covered a stretch of overs where Kingsport were trying to turn pressure into a collapse. The pair took Westhaven close to the hundred mark, and that was the point where the innings had its one real chance to become solid. Nathan Cole shut that chance down. Elliot was caught by Evan Blake, and Finn Mercer was bowled almost immediately afterward, which changed the whole mood of the afternoon. One moment Westhaven were slow but still structured; the next, they were five down with Daniel Moss left trying to patch another hole. George Nolan came in and gave the lower half of the innings its stubborn thread. His unbeaten twenty-two was not flashy, but in this kind of innings it mattered because he refused to let the tail vanish without taking the score upward. Daniel Moss stayed long enough to reach forty-four before Ryan Grant removed him, and that wicket finally took away Westhaven’s main anchor. After that it was a series of small resistances: Henry Cross stayed for nine, Isaac Dale scratched out a few, Jack Holden found one boundary, and Leo Barrett hung around just long enough for George to add a last handful. None of those stands became a true finish, yet collectively they dragged Westhaven to one hundred and sixty-four all out in the forty-ninth over. That total was not empty, but it was built in fragments rather than authority. Daniel Moss held the innings together, Callum Reed gave it the best top-order spark, George Nolan kept it alive at the end, and the extras column, especially the wides and leg-side leakage, carried enough weight to make the final score look healthier than the batting alone had earned. Kingsport’s bowling was not about one spectacular burst only. It was layered. Connor Shaw opened the first door, Nathan Cole struck through the top and middle and produced the double blow that changed the score’s meaning, Ryan Grant took three while keeping the back end under control, Mason Hart’s spell was almost quietly brutal because he gave up so little across ten overs, Dylan Price did not get a wicket but helped keep the middle passage from loosening, and Logan Wells closed the innings with the final lbw. The important thing was timing. Every time Westhaven found a pair that seemed capable of giving the innings shape, Kingsport found the wicket before the partnership could become the day’s main story. Then the chase began under D/L conditions, and whatever tension Westhaven hoped the adjusted target might create disappeared quickly. Isaac Rowan and Luke Bennett came out with the correct balance of certainty and restraint. They did not bat as if the match had already been gifted to them; they batted as if they understood that the only way Westhaven could re-enter the contest was through a careless early mistake. Isaac was the sharper presence from the start, pushing hard through the off side, taking anything short, and then growing into a rhythm where almost every loose ball seemed to find the rope. His unbeaten sixty-three from fifty-three balls was clean, controlled and decisive, and it deserved the match award because he did not merely finish the chase, he set the tone that made Westhaven’s defence look thin almost from the opening spell. Luke Bennett was just as important beside him. His unbeaten fifty-five from forty-five balls kept the partnership balanced, and that balance removed Westhaven’s last tactical option. They could not simply bowl at Isaac and hope Luke would stall. Luke kept finding his own boundaries, kept matching the tempo, and kept the score moving whenever Isaac had just taken the larger share of an over. Westhaven’s bowlers had no real breakthrough route. Henry Cross was tidy in his first spell and even found a maiden, but without a wicket it only delayed the chase rather than altered it. Isaac Dale and Leo Barrett leaked too quickly. Callum Reed tried to change the angle and still found the openers ready. Jack Holden worked through the longest spell of the defence, but by then the partnership had already shifted from promising to inevitable. Only three extras came in the chase, which made the contrast with Westhaven’s innings even sharper. Kingsport were not relying on the fielding side’s mistakes. They were taking the game themselves. By the time the partnership passed three figures, the match had lost the last of its tension, and when Kingsport reached one hundred and twenty-one without loss in the seventeenth over, the official D/L result simply confirmed the cricket that had been visible for a long while. Westhaven had spent nearly fifty overs scraping together a total made of slow repairs, isolated contributions and too many extras. Kingsport needed only two openers, sixteen overs and a little more, and almost no charity. That is why the ten-wicket margin felt completely justified. Westhaven were not humiliated in the first innings, but they were never allowed to control it. Kingsport were never hurried in the reply, never trapped by the target, and never forced into the kind of middle-order examination that Westhaven desperately needed. Isaac Rowan’s award was right because his unbeaten hand made the chase look like a procession, but the win itself was broader: Nathan Cole and Ryan Grant broke the first innings at the right times, Mason Hart tightened the middle, Logan Wells ended the tail, and Isaac Rowan with Luke Bennett converted a potentially awkward D/L chase into a clean match statement.
