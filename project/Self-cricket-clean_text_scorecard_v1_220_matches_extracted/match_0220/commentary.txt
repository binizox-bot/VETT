Brookfield Park in Riverton had that dry match feel where the outfield promised runs but the pitch still asked enough questions to punish anyone who started lazily, and Westbridge, having chosen to bat first, spent most of their innings showing how difficult it can be to build a score that is useful without ever fully becoming dominant. Arjun Hale and Mason Quill gave them the first platform, not a reckless launch, not a highlight reel start, more the kind of opening stand that slowly removes the new ball from the conversation. Arjun was the more assertive of the two, strong when Ridgeford overpitched and brave enough to take on the line once he had seen the bounce, while Mason played the longer, quieter hand, taking the awkward overs and making sure the score moved even when the field was set correctly. Their stand carried Westbridge into the late side of the first power phase, and although there were already some extra runs scattered through the score, the main point was that Ridgeford had not found the early incision they wanted. Finley Grant finally removed Arjun, and that wicket let Ridgeford reset the field for a while. Noah Crest came in and helped Mason keep the innings moving, but Julian Stone’s spell began to change the texture of the match. He had Mason stumped after a patient forty-six, then trapped Owen Vale soon after Owen had tried to punch the tempo forward, and suddenly Westbridge were not in trouble exactly, but they were no longer strolling. Noah and Nathan Reed then built the second important phase, and this is where the innings found its real centre. Noah’s contribution was practical, a bridge between the set opener and the middle order, but Nathan was the player who made the innings hold together after every small break. He did not arrive with the freedom of a death-overs hitter. He had to read Ridgeford’s best spell, manage Julian Stone’s control, and keep Westbridge from letting a good start become merely average. When Finley Grant bowled Noah, Nathan simply shifted into the next version of the innings, this time with Quentin Ford beside him. Quentin’s twelve was not large, yet it mattered because it kept Nathan from having to attack every ball. Their partnership pushed Westbridge past the danger zone and into a score that Ridgeford would have to respect. Julian Stone returned to pull the innings back again, removing Quentin and Riley Moss in the same pressure stretch, and Ridgeford briefly had the chance to hold Westbridge somewhere close to the low two-hundreds. Nathan prevented that. He kept reconnecting the card through Sawyer Beck’s short cameo, through Theo Lane’s slower support, and through the little late fragments that kept the total moving even after the innings had become ragged. His run-out near the end left the scorecard slightly unfinished emotionally, because he had been the one batter capable of giving Westbridge a cleaner final push, but by then his seventy-three had already done the main work. Victor Shaw and Theo Lane could only add small late pieces before the innings closed at two hundred and forty-eight in the fiftieth over. Ridgeford’s bowling had clear value, especially Julian Stone’s four-wicket spell and Finley Grant’s early control, but their problem was leakage around the pressure: wides, no-balls, expensive short spells and enough small gifts to let Westbridge survive every time the innings seemed ready to tighten. The chase asked Ridgeford for a measured start, and they did not get one. Carter Lane tried to play the captain’s holding role but fell to Sawyer Beck before the opening stand could become meaningful. Ethan Rowe followed to Quentin Ford, and when Wyatt Brooks pinned Adrian Lowe after a slow, uncomfortable stay, Ridgeford were already three down with the innings needing a repair instead of a chase. Dominic Shaw and Ben Hartley gave them that repair, and for a while it was the best batting passage Ridgeford produced all day. Ben was the cleaner support player, finding enough scoring options to stop Westbridge from putting everyone around the bat, while Dominic took the longer role, working through the difficult balls and allowing the required work to stay inside the bounds of possibility. Their stand of sixty-eight changed the tone from early damage to cautious belief. It did not make Ridgeford favourites, but it made Westbridge bowl properly again. Victor Shaw’s wicket of Ben was therefore one of the decisive moments, because it broke the only partnership that had looked capable of turning the chase into structure. Dominic tried to continue with Finn Barrett, and that smaller stand kept Ridgeford alive on the board, but Noah Crest’s caught-and-bowled dismissal of Dominic changed the chase sharply. From there Ridgeford had batters, but not a centre. Isaac Rowe went quickly, Finn was taken by Wyatt Brooks, and although Gavin Price gave the lower order a useful punch, the chase was beginning to fragment into little pieces rather than rebuild into one final push. Theo Lane then removed Gavin and Julian Stone, cutting off the last practical route into a tense finish, and Quentin Ford returned to close it by removing Harvey Cole. Ridgeford were all out for one hundred and eighty-seven, short by sixty-one. The result was not created by one overwhelming spell in the second innings. It came from Westbridge taking wickets at the right phase again and again: Sawyer Beck opening the door, Quentin Ford cutting early and late, Wyatt Brooks breaking Adrian and Finn, Noah Crest removing Dominic and Isaac, Victor Shaw ending the best stand, and Theo Lane denying the tail a final argument. That distributed pressure explains why Nathan Reed’s player-of-the-match award fits cleanly. Julian Stone had the best individual bowling figures, Dominic Shaw and Ben Hartley made Ridgeford’s only convincing repair, but Nathan’s seventy-three was the innings that gave Westbridge a total with enough depth. He turned a platform into a defensible score, held the middle together after repeated interruptions, and made sure Ridgeford were chasing a target that required more than one partnership. In the end Westbridge did not dominate every phase; they simply owned more of the important ones, and that was enough to turn a balanced contest into a clear sixty-one-run win.
