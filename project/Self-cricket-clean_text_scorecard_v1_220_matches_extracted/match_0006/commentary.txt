Brookfield Oval in Eastmere had that slightly slow feel from the first inspection, the kind of surface where a batting side can build something useful if it accepts hard work, but where fluent scoring never quite arrives for free, so Bayside XI’s decision to bat first made sense on paper and still came with immediate risk once Ridgeway XI began landing the ball into the surface rather than floating it up for easy drives. Ari Cole gave Bayside the first movement of the morning, not in a reckless way, more through controlled boundary strokes whenever Elliot Shaw or Caleb Frost missed wide enough to let him free the hands, while Jonah Miles played the quieter end, leaving more than he scored and trying to make the new ball old enough for the middle order to take over. That opening partnership gave Bayside a workable base, but it never felt dominant because Ridgeway kept the ring tight and made almost every single feel negotiated. Shaw eventually removed Ari just as the start was beginning to look properly useful, and that first wicket changed the tone because Bayside had not raced away, they had only survived into a better position. Caleb Warren then gave the innings its most important shape. He was the one batter who looked capable of turning occupation into control, first alongside Jonah, then more convincingly with Finn Carter, who did not score heavily but stayed long enough to let the captain settle into the rhythm of the pitch. Warren’s innings was not a free-flowing fifty, and that is exactly why it mattered; he had to build it through patience, nudged singles, controlled drives and the occasional heavier shot when Ridgeway overcorrected. The stand with Finn became the best part of Bayside’s innings, though even there the total never took on the feel of a side charging toward something large. Ridgeway’s attack kept making quiet interventions. Ryan Vale removed Finn, Sam Brooks cut off Luca Reed before he could give the middle order a clean bridge, and when Warren himself fell with the score still short of the kind of platform Bayside wanted, the innings began to look more like a collection of useful pieces than one complete statement. Owen Hart and Tyler Cross then gave Bayside their second practical passage, not spectacular, but valuable enough to keep the innings moving through the forties of the over count. Owen did the longer repair work, Tyler added the slightly sharper edge, and for a while Bayside seemed likely to push beyond the low two-hundreds into a more uncomfortable chase. Noah Lane and Sam Brooks made sure that did not happen cleanly. Owen went before the final push could fully form, Tyler followed after a short burst, and Leo Marsh was bowled before the tail could reset. Zane Foster’s not-out hand gave the innings its last bit of punch, and Milo Grant’s small finish mattered because it pushed Bayside to 211 for eight rather than something visibly under par, but Ridgeway walked off knowing they had kept the first innings in reach. The bowling card showed that shared pressure well: Ryan Vale, Brooks and Lane all took pairs of wickets, Shaw and Frost supplied the earlier cuts, and Mason Vale’s few overs were expensive enough to remind everyone that the surface could still be scored from if a batter found his tempo. Chasing 212, Ridgeway needed discipline more than bravado, and Bayside briefly made the target feel trickier than it looked. Oliver Reed was pinned early, Theo Grant countered with enough intent to keep the score moving, but Owen Hart removed him too, leaving Ridgeway two down with the chase still young and enough overs remaining for Bayside to believe a squeeze was possible. That was where Mason Vale began to take ownership of the match. He did not walk in and finish the game in a rush. He first gave the innings shape, then tempo, then authority. Connor Wells helped him through the most important rebuilding passage, playing the captain’s role with restraint, taking time out of the game and preventing Bayside from attacking Mason with an entirely closed field. Their stand was the largest of the chase and the first real shift in Ridgeway’s favour, moving the score from a slightly awkward position into a place where the target started to feel like a calculation rather than a threat. Caleb Warren eventually bowled Wells, which gave Bayside a route back, and when Dylan Stone also fell after helping Mason move the chase through the next phase, the match was still not finished. Ridgeway had wickets in hand but not enough control to be careless. Ryan Wells came and went, Sam Brooks offered a useful late burst, and by the time Lucas Brooks bowled him at 189 for six, Bayside had created the one final moment they needed if the game was going to turn. The issue for them was that Mason Vale was still there. He had already absorbed the early repair work, already handled the middle-overs pressure, and now he read the finish with the calm of a batter who knew exactly how many risks were actually required. Liam Stone joined him and did the simple things correctly, blocking the good ball, taking the run when it was there, and giving Mason the strike often enough for the chase to keep shrinking. Mason’s unbeaten 82 was the innings that joined every part of Ridgeway’s reply together: the rescue after two wickets, the measured rebuild with Wells, the controlled progress with Stone, the response to late wickets, and finally the finishing push. Ridgeway reached 214 for six in the forty-sixth over, winning by four wickets, and the result felt fair because Bayside had enough batting depth to post something competitive but not enough of a defining partnership to make it heavy, while Ridgeway had the one decisive hand in Mason Vale and enough supporting pieces around him to keep the chase from breaking. Bayside’s bowlers shared wickets and kept the match alive longer than the final chase might suggest, but Ridgeway had the clearer match-winner, and in a game where neither innings fully ran away, Mason’s ability to stay, absorb, accelerate and close was the difference.
