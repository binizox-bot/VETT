Good afternoon from Northbank Oval in Greendale, where Stoneford chose to field first and for almost half the innings had just enough wickets in hand, just enough interruptions on the card, to believe Ridgefield might be held to something manageable, but the longer the day went the clearer it became that Ridgefield had two innings hidden inside one: the first built around Ethan Marsh’s captain’s hundred, the second dragged into heavy territory by Nathan Vale’s unbeaten control through the lower order, and once those two pieces had been stitched together the chase became less a pursuit than a long wait for the collapse that Nathan himself would later deliver with the ball. The opening phase already gave Stoneford a warning, even though they did take the first wicket inside the seventh over. Caleb Ward never really moved beyond survival mode, and Julian Cross eventually had him taken by Blake Turner, but Ethan Marsh was scoring at the other end with the confidence of a batter who had decided very early that loose balls would not be allowed to pass as dots. He was clean through the off side, strong when the seamers overpitched, and sharp enough against anything into the body to keep the fielders moving rather than setting into easy catching positions. Dylan Frost then came in and played the kind of supporting stay that does not look powerful on the page but matters because it gives the dominant batter time to keep shaping the innings. Dylan made only nine, yet the stand with Ethan still added another useful block, and by the time Connor Hayes had him caught by Lucas Ford, Ridgefield had already moved beyond the danger of a false start. Stoneford did pull the innings back for a while. Owen Doyle came and went without settling. Finn Shaw also struggled to get the ball away, and when Connor Hayes picked him off after Harrison Pike had already removed Owen, the score had the slightly unstable feel of a side depending too much on one batter. Ethan was still there, though, and that changed everything. He kept the innings upright through the awkward passage, turned singles into pressure, and then, when Nathan Vale arrived, the whole match began to shift from an Ethan-led platform into a two-man construction. Ethan eventually reached a hundred that deserved every bit of its weight, not because it was a flawless procession, but because it had to carry multiple partners through uneven spells and still remain aggressive enough to stop Stoneford from settling. Elliot Marsh finally bowled him for one hundred and four, and at that point Stoneford had a narrow opening. Ridgefield were five down. The captain was gone. The lower middle order had not yet proved it could finish. That opening lasted only briefly because Nathan Vale read the situation with unusual clarity. He did not start by swinging at everything. He first made sure the innings survived Ethan’s departure, then adjusted as the wickets around him kept coming. Hayden Cole gave him a short stand before Connor Hayes bowled him. Isaac Flynn came in and punched the ball cleanly enough to add a brisk eighteen before Julian Cross ran him out. Jacob Hart hung around long enough to let Nathan reset again. These were not glamorous contributions, but each one gave Nathan another few overs, another few balls to assess the death phase, another chance to move Ridgefield away from a merely decent total. The real break came with Logan Pierce. Logan’s own nine was not large, yet his partnership with Nathan changed the whole scorecard. That ninth-wicket stand was the part Stoneford will regret most, because the innings should have been closing down and instead expanded sharply. Nathan controlled the strike better and better, waited for Stoneford to miss their length, then punished the fuller ball, the slower ball that sat up, and anything wide enough to be cut behind point. Logan kept surviving, kept taking the small runs, and that was all Nathan needed. The score moved from the low two hundreds into a place where the chase would demand something extraordinary. Even when Logan was finally run out, Nathan still had one last burst beside Mason Reid, finishing unbeaten on one hundred and two from eighty-nine deliveries, the kind of lower-order century that does more than pad a score because it changes how the second innings is played before the first ball is even bowled. Ridgefield closed on two hundred and ninety-two in the fiftieth over, and Stoneford’s bowling card was left looking strangely mixed: there were wickets, yes, and Connor Hayes had done real work through the middle, Harrison Pike was tidy and effective, Elliot Marsh removed the two bookend wickets that mattered on paper, Julian Cross made the early cut, but nobody could stop both Ethan and Nathan in the phases that mattered. The extras helped too, especially the wides that kept softening Stoneford’s pressure, though this was not a total gifted by indiscipline alone. Ridgefield had two batters who controlled the match from different points, and Stoneford never found a full spell that could contain both. Chasing two hundred and ninety-three, Stoneford needed a stable opening, a patient middle, and at least one player capable of batting deep. They got none of those things in a form that lasted. Brennan Turner gave the innings one early spark, one blow over the rope and a few signs that the new ball could be challenged, but Mason Reid removed him before the opening stand could become a real platform. Arlo Wells, the wicketkeeper, stayed longer but never looked fluent. He was caught in that uncomfortable zone where survival uses up balls without loosening the field, and every over he remained there seemed to increase the pressure on the next batter rather than reduce it. Connor Hayes tried to stay with him and was caught and bowled by Mason Reid, leaving Stoneford already two down with the chase barely formed. Nathan Vale then entered with the ball and turned the match into a dismantling. Damon Ward, the captain, was bowled before he could create any calm. Arlo’s long struggle ended with Caleb Ward taking the catch. Elliot Marsh then followed, Ethan Marsh holding the chance, and suddenly the chase had no shape at all. It was not simply that wickets were falling. It was the type of innings Stoneford were being forced into: no one was scoring quickly enough to make Ridgefield spread out, no one was surviving with enough authority to make the bowlers change plans, and the few free runs from wides did nothing more than delay the arithmetic. Felix Grant lasted fourteen balls for nothing, and when Mason Reid bowled him the card already looked broken. Harrison Pike briefly swung hard, striking one boundary and giving the innings a tiny shock of movement, but Nathan bowled him almost immediately. Isaac Dale went next, taken by the substitute Noah Grant, and at forty-one for eight Stoneford were not chasing the target anymore; they were trying to avoid total humiliation. Kieran Moss was the only batter who gave the lower order any visible fight. His seventeen from fourteen included two clean sixes, and for a short passage with Julian Cross he dragged the score upward in a way that at least made Ridgefield finish the job properly. That partnership was Stoneford’s best of the innings, which says plenty about the top order. Kieran’s counterpunch did not alter the match, but it did briefly change the sound around it, a couple of shots finally struck with conviction, a little resistance arriving far too late. Mason Reid ended that by bowling Kieran, and Nathan Vale then completed the innings by removing Julian Cross, Stoneford all out for sixty-three in the twenty-first over. The chase was brutal in its simplicity. Mason Reid took four wickets and did the opening damage. Nathan Vale took six and owned everything after that. Jacob Hart’s short spell gave Ridgefield a bridge between the two, but in truth the match was carried by the two wicket-takers and the pressure of the enormous target behind them. Stoneford’s batting card had only two real flickers: Brennan’s early eleven and Kieran’s late seventeen. Arlo’s five from a long stay showed how difficult it was to get started, and the rest of the lineup almost disappeared into single figures. Extras reached double figures again, which made the total look a touch less severe than the batting deserved, but nothing about the chase suggested a side capable of threatening Ridgefield’s score. That is why Nathan Vale’s player-of-the-match award is not merely defensible, it is unavoidable. Ethan Marsh’s hundred gave Ridgefield the first half of the innings and was a superb captain’s contribution, but Nathan’s unbeaten century at the back end transformed the match from competitive to crushing, and his six wickets then removed any possible argument in the chase. Ridgefield won by two hundred and twenty-nine runs because they owned both pressure points: the moment after Ethan fell, when Nathan turned vulnerability into acceleration, and the early-middle section of the chase, when Nathan and Mason made Stoneford restart again and again until there was nothing left to restart. By the close, Stoneford’s decision to field first looked like a memory from a completely different match. Ridgefield had absorbed the early wickets, built through Ethan, finished through Nathan, and then defended with such force that the result line felt less like exaggeration than a precise summary of how one-sided the decisive passages had been.
