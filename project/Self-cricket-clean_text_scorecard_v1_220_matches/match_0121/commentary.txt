At Redstone Cricket Ground in Mirrendale, Southvale won the toss and chose to field, and for the first few overs that decision did not look unreasonable, because Duneford came out with obvious intent but also with just enough risk in their approach to give the bowling side something to attack. Nolan Pierce was the player who gave the innings its early heat. He did not waste time feeling for the surface, instead going hard at anything that offered width or length, and his twenty from nine balls, with three fours and a six, immediately forced Southvale’s new-ball pair to defend more space than they wanted. Aiden Ross played the calmer opening role beside him, not passive, but less explosive, making sure the innings still had shape when Nolan was doing the louder work. Xavier Holt eventually broke through by having Nolan caught by Sawyer Grant, and at twenty-nine for one Southvale had ended the first burst before it became a runaway start. What followed was more important for Duneford than the initial flash. Evan Vale came in and began the innings that made the whole batting card coherent. He did not copy Nolan’s tempo. He settled, watched the pace, and started building in a way that gave Duneford a center rather than just a fast opening. Aiden was valuable in that phase because he had already seen the new ball and could shift into a more active supporting role. Together Aiden and Evan added sixty-seven, the best stand of the innings, and it gave Duneford the base they needed to go beyond a merely busy powerplay. Aiden’s thirty-six had enough boundaries and enough intent to keep Southvale from squeezing, while Evan’s scoring was more measured, more durable, and more important in the long shape of the innings. Zack Dean did eventually remove Aiden, stumped by Theo Cole, and at ninety-six for two Southvale still had a chance to keep the innings in a controllable range if they could strike quickly again. Marcus Reid made sure they could not. The captain came in with a clear sense of the match situation: Evan had done the platform work, and now the innings needed force without losing its line. Marcus did not explode immediately, but he changed the pressure in the back half because he was willing to take on the fuller ball and hard enough on anything in the slot to make the field drop deeper. His partnership with Evan added forty-nine and linked the middle overs to the death overs. Evan, by then, had moved past fifty and was playing the exact kind of innings that holds a total together across different phases: he had absorbed pressure, built with one partner, shifted gears with another, and still stayed long enough to make the last five overs meaningful. Warren Blake finally trapped him lbw for sixty-four, and that was an important wicket, but it came late enough that Duneford already had the structure of a strong total. Kieran Moss arrived for a short, sharp cameo, taking eleven from five balls before Warren removed him as well, and then Marcus used the last couple of deliveries to push the innings out to one hundred and eighty-one for four. That final number mattered because the innings had been layered: Nolan’s punch, Aiden’s support, Evan’s control, Marcus’ finish, and Kieran’s brief late strike. Southvale had bowling positives, especially Xavier’s early control, Zack Dean’s tidy spell, and Warren’s two wickets, but Oscar Kent’s expensive four overs and the inability to break the key middle stand early enough left them chasing more than they would have wanted. Southvale’s reply then started in the worst possible way for a side needing one hundred and eighty-two. Oscar Kent struck one six but was bowled by Sawyer Flynn before the opening stand could become real. Aaron Blake was caught off Riley Hayes soon after, and when Quentin Hale fell to Quentin Brooks with the score at twenty-five, Southvale were three down and already in the kind of hole that makes a big chase feel twice as large. At that point, Duneford had almost everything they wanted: wickets, scoreboard pressure, and a batting side forced to rebuild while the asking rate kept pushing forward. Then Theo Cole and Parker Shaw changed the game. The fourth-wicket stand was worth ninety-three, and it turned the chase from a recovery mission into a genuine threat. Theo was the more balanced hand, reaching fifty-two with four fours and two sixes, giving the innings both scoring and stability. Parker was sharper, more dangerous, and more disruptive to Duneford’s plans, forty-eight from twenty-seven with three fours and three sixes, the kind of captain’s counterattack that makes a fielding side rethink every matchup. For several overs, Southvale looked as if they had not merely repaired the chase but seized it. The required rate stopped feeling dangerous. The field spread. Duneford’s bowlers had to search for answers rather than simply apply pressure. Sawyer Flynn finally found the break by bowling Parker at one hundred and eighteen, and that wicket changed the chase’s emotional direction. Evan Vale then delivered the next key strike, trapping Theo lbw, and suddenly the two batters who had built Southvale’s winning position were both gone. Evan’s value to the match became clear in that passage. His batting had given Duneford the total, but his bowling now removed the man who had made the chase feel stable. He then took a return catch to dismiss Sawyer Grant, and in the space of a short spell Southvale had slipped from apparent control into a new scramble. Riley Brooks refused to let the match end there. His unbeaten thirty-six was an excellent late hand, full of firm driving and calm enough running to keep the chase alive. Warren Blake gave him real support for a while, adding eleven and helping put on thirty-four, and that stand dragged Southvale back into the contest after the double blow. Riley kept finding enough boundary options to make Duneford nervous, and the match moved deep enough that every single mattered. Riley Hayes broke the stand by removing Warren, and Quentin Brooks then bowled Xavier Holt, leaving Southvale eight down and still needing a final push from Riley with Zack Dean beside him. The last stand added ten, not enough to win but enough to make the finish genuinely tense. Riley stayed there to the end, still trying to manufacture the one final scoring blow that would turn the match, but Southvale closed on one hundred and seventy-eight for eight, three runs short. The result felt narrow because it was narrow, but it also felt earned by Duneford because they kept finding the right intervention after being placed under severe pressure. They were ahead after the early wickets, behind in mood during the Theo-Parker stand, back in control after Evan and Sawyer struck, and then under pressure again when Riley dragged the chase to the edge. Evan Vale as Player of the Match fits the structure cleanly. His sixty-four was the innings that connected Duneford’s start to their finish, and his wickets of Theo and Sawyer struck directly at Southvale’s chase when the match was most dangerous. Marcus Reid’s late forty-three was crucial, Nolan Pierce created the early tempo, and Sawyer Flynn and Riley Hayes both delivered major bowling moments. Southvale had enough quality to nearly overturn a terrible start, especially through Theo, Parker, and Riley, but they were always one boundary or one partnership short. Duneford won because their total had just enough depth, and because their bowlers, after being hit hard through the middle, found the three or four balls that mattered most.
