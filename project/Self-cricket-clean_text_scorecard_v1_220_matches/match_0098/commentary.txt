At Riverside Oval in the East Midlands, Harborview chose to bat first and produced the kind of innings that grows more impressive when you look at how cleanly each phase handed the game to the next, because this was not a flat-out blast from the first ball, and it was not a rescue from a deep collapse either; it was a carefully layered batting performance in which Adrian Cole stayed as the central figure while different partners changed the tempo around him. The first stand with Liam Foster gave Harborview exactly the platform they wanted. Liam was the more obvious early aggressor in flashes, driving neatly when Arun Malik overpitched and pulling whenever the length sat up, while Adrian played with a slightly broader sense of time, taking the safer singles, waiting on the slower ball, and then opening his shoulders once the field began to spread. The opening partnership reached sixty-eight before Liam fell, and that was already enough to put Stonebridge into a reactive position, because a side defending the ball first wants wickets before the batting order settles, not one opener set and the other already past a brisk start. Arun did finally remove Liam, and Dev Patel followed by stumping Noah Blake after a short second-wicket passage, but those wickets did not really reverse the innings. They only slowed one part of it. Adrian was still there, already reading the pace well, and Ethan Ward gave him just enough of a bridge to carry the innings through the middle. Ethan’s twelve was modest, yet in this sort of innings a small contribution can still have value if it prevents two quick wickets from becoming a proper squeeze. Kiran Shah bowled Ethan with the total at one-ten, and that left Stonebridge with a chance to believe they could still drag Harborview back toward the mid-150s. Ryan Bell changed that calculation almost immediately. His thirty-five from sixteen balls was the loudest piece of batting on the card, two fours, three sixes, and almost no dead time, and the stand with Adrian was the stretch where the innings stopped being simply strong and became genuinely heavy. Adrian did not have to dominate every ball in that partnership. That was the point. He had already built the platform, and now he could let Bell’s hitting stretch the field while he kept picking the gaps around it. Their forty-eight-run stand pushed the total to one-fifty-eight, and even when Adrian finally fell for seventy-three, caught by Nolan Pierce off Dev Patel, he had already done the complete top-order job: survive the first phase, own the middle, and stay long enough for the finisher to attack from a secure base. Bell was removed soon after, but the damage did not stop there. Mason Reed and Caleb Hart squeezed the last few balls hard enough to make the total feel bigger than it had looked only an over earlier, Mason finishing unbeaten on eleven, Caleb clearing the rope in a brief three-ball stay, and Harborview closing at one-eighty-one for five. That final number was built from proper batting more than charity. Thirteen extras helped, but the innings stood mainly on Adrian’s long hand, Liam’s early push, Bell’s acceleration, and the small last-over bite from Mason and Caleb. Stonebridge had useful spells in isolation. Dev Patel’s two wickets gave them the best return, Arun Malik broke the opening stand, Kiran Shah held parts of the middle together, and Brennan Cole eventually removed Bell, but the deeper problem was that none of those wickets came in a cluster large enough to actually take the innings away from Harborview. Chasing one-eighty-two, Stonebridge needed either a clean powerplay or one major innings to hold the reply together. They got neither. Leo Grant was bowled by Sam Porter before the chase could breathe, Harun Ellis was then bowled by William Hart, and Nolan Pierce, after trying to occupy one end, was caught behind off Daniel Vale with the score still only in the mid-twenties. Milo Stone briefly tried to give the innings some shape, but Daniel Vale bowled him before he could turn that stay into a platform, and at thirty for four the chase had already changed from pursuit into repair. Sahil Khan and Omar Reid added a little resistance, but Sahil’s dismissal to Adrian Cole was an important moment because it cut away the captain before he could play the supporting role Omar needed. Omar was the only Stonebridge batter who really threatened to change the tone. His twenty-nine from twenty balls had enough force to remind Harborview that the game was not completely finished, and with Ishaan Patel he put together the one partnership that looked capable of causing a delayed problem. That stand reached twenty-nine, and for a few overs the chase had just enough noise to make Harborview reset their fields. Daniel Vale ended that possibility by removing Omar at seventy-four for six, and that was the wicket that truly ended the contest in practical terms. Ishaan followed to William Hart, Arun Malik was caught off Julian Reed, Brennan Cole was bowled without scoring, and although Kiran Shah and Dev Patel survived the last few balls, survival was all it was. Stonebridge finished on ninety-nine for nine, well short of the target, and the way they got there explains the scale of the defeat: no top-order platform, no half-century stand, no batter able to stay long enough after Omar’s cameo to keep the rate relevant. Harborview’s bowlers shared the work cleanly. Daniel Vale’s three wickets broke the chase at three different points, Sam Porter’s early wicket and tight spell kept the start narrow, William Hart removed two batters who were trying to rebuild, Julian Reed handled the lower order, and Adrian Cole’s wicket of Sahil Khan added the all-round touch to an already match-defining batting performance. That is why the player-of-the-match decision is straightforward. Adrian’s seventy-three was the spine of Harborview’s innings, and the wicket he added later came at a useful point in the chase. Harborview won by eighty-two runs because their batting had sequence and their bowling had timing. Stonebridge had one small middle-order flare through Omar Reid, but no second phase to support it, and once the chase lost shape before the halfway mark, the result became a matter of margin rather than direction.
