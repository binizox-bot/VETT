Good afternoon from Southbank Oval in Fairhaven, where Fairmont chose to bat and ended up producing the kind of innings that feels ordinary only if you glance at the final total too quickly, because their 318 all out was not built in one smooth climb but in a set of sharply different phases: a controlled start, a quick captain’s lift, a long middle repair, and then a late burst so violent that Westmere were left chasing not just a large score but the emotional damage of having watched the innings get away after they had created several chances to pull it back. Luca Ford and Aiden Carter gave Fairmont a steady enough beginning, not a spectacular one, but a useful one, because the first job after choosing to bat was to prevent Westmere from turning the new ball into a sequence of early breakthroughs. Luca made only eight before Benjamin Shaw removed him, but by then Aiden had already begun the slow work of giving the innings a spine. Noah Ellis arrived and briefly changed the tempo, striking two boundaries and two sixes in a rapid twenty-six, and while his stay was short, it had real value because it stopped Westmere from settling into a post-wicket squeeze. When Noah fell, Fairmont were still only in the first act of their innings, and the real centre came through Aiden Carter and Owen Vale. Their partnership of ninety-one was the most important structural stand of the day. Aiden was the long hand, careful without being frozen, able to absorb the slower overs and keep the innings alive while Westmere tried to deny pace. Owen supplied the slightly stronger scoring movement, and his fifty-two gave the stand the weight it needed, because without his boundary threat Fairmont might have drifted into a holding pattern rather than building a platform. Caleb Ward finally bowled Owen, and Aaron Blake later removed Aiden for fifty-seven, but those wickets came after Fairmont had already crossed into a position from which the lower order could attack rather than merely survive. Parker Shaw helped bridge that awkward middle-late section, first with Aiden and then briefly with Quinn Harper, and although the score wobbled with wickets, the innings never truly folded. Then Riley Bennett entered and changed the match in barely more than two overs. His thirty-eight from twelve balls was the purest burst of the innings, three fours, four sixes, and far more importantly a complete change in scale. Before that cameo, Fairmont were looking at a strong total. After it, Westmere were staring at something that already felt oppressive. Riley’s stand with Parker was worth forty, and almost all of its force came from his bat, the kind of short innings that leaves bowlers suddenly missing length, captains dragging fielders around, and every misfield feeling larger than it should. Even when Riley fell, Fairmont did not lose the late charge. Theo Grant and Victor Lane gave the innings its second finishing wave, adding fifty-six for the ninth wicket, Victor hammering thirty-one from twenty and Theo carrying the finish with a composed but fast forty-six. That partnership turned the final overs from useful into punishing, and the last pair still managed another small push before Theo was out with the total on 318. Westmere had moments with the ball. Benjamin Shaw’s early double strike mattered. Aaron Blake took three wickets and prevented a completely unrestrained finish. Caleb Ward got Owen Vale at a useful time. Harvey Cole removed Riley just when the game threatened to disappear even faster. But the wider problem was that Westmere kept breaking one stand only to concede the next phase, and their twenty-seven extras, especially the wides, kept giving Fairmont movement even in overs where the batting was not fully on top. Chasing 319, Westmere needed a strong, clean start and got the opposite. Riley Bennett removed Aaron Blake early, Parker Hayes trapped Benjamin Shaw, and Riley then bowled Caleb Ward, leaving the chase almost immediately in repair mode. Dominic Wells and Elliot Price produced the first meaningful resistance, a stand of thirty that at least stopped the innings from becoming a straight collapse, but it was never fast enough to disturb Fairmont. Elliot’s seven from thirty-two showed how difficult it was to find tempo, and Dominic, while more productive, was still operating inside pressure rather than outside it. Victor Lane broke that stand, then took Dominic himself, and when Gavin Stone was pinned first ball the chase had fallen to fifty-four for six. From there, the result was no longer seriously in question. Finley Grant and Harvey Cole tried to stretch the innings, and later Harvey worked with Isaac Rowe and then Jack Mercer to give Westmere some lower-order dignity, but the structure of the chase had already been lost. Harvey’s thirty was the most substantial batting effort, Isaac’s eight and Jack’s sixteen added some resistance, and the extras column became enormous, thirty-six in total, which made the score look healthier than the batting really deserved. Fairmont were not perfect in discipline, but they kept taking wickets at the exact points where Westmere needed a partnership to become something more than delay. Riley Bennett’s three wickets gave the chase its first deep wound. Victor Lane’s three wickets ripped out the middle. Quinn Harper, Owen Vale and Wyatt Cole each added a wicket to stop the lower-order stands from growing too far. Westmere were eventually dismissed for 128 in the thirty-ninth over, and the margin of 190 runs felt completely aligned with the cricket. Fairmont had the stronger structure with the bat, the more explosive late hitters, and the better-timed breakthroughs with the ball. Westmere had fragments: Dominic’s attempt to stabilize, Harvey’s long resistance, Jack’s late push, and scattered bowling moments. They never had a phase large enough to challenge the shape of the match. Riley Bennett was the right player of the match because his contribution sat at the two most important pressure points: first, the late batting burst that transformed Fairmont’s total from strong to crushing, and then the early wickets that made sure Westmere’s chase never had time to become a real pursuit.
