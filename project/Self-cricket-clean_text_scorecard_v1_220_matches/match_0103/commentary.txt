Riverside Cricket Ground in the East Midlands gave this final the kind of shape that makes a chase look straightforward only after it has been completed, because Meadowbrook were invited to bat first, stumbled badly through the first half, then found one captain’s innings strong enough to drag them into a competitive position, while Harborline answered with a chase that began in a rush, briefly lost its balance, and then settled around the one player who had already shaped the match with the ball. Meadowbrook’s start was never clean. Toby Reed found a couple of early scoring shots and Elliot Marsh stayed long enough to make the opening passage look survivable, but the stand was already leaning on extras before Ryan Cole found Reed’s edge and Blake Porter took the catch. Cameron Vale was then bowled by Victor Hale before he could get moving, and when Elliot Marsh followed soon after, caught off Adam Pierce, Meadowbrook had reached only the mid-forties with three wickets gone and the innings already asking Julian Brooks to do more than simply bat through. Harvey Stone tried to give him a little breathing room, but Victor Hale returned to trap him in front, and at sixty-seven for four the innings had that unstable look where one more wicket could have turned a final into a one-sided defence of something far too light. Julian was the reason that did not happen. He began by absorbing the pressure rather than trying to hit his way out of it, then slowly turned the scoring back in Meadowbrook’s favour. Owen Clarke gave him the first properly aggressive support, twenty-four from seventeen with a couple of clean blows over the rope, and their stand of thirty-nine was the passage that moved Meadowbrook from danger into a total with some spine. Ryan Cole did bowl Owen before the partnership could become decisive, but Julian was now set, and that changed the value of every remaining over. Mason Trent added a useful seven in the next stand, more important for the time and strike rotation than for the raw number, while Julian began to attack the spaces Harborline had been trying to protect. His unbeaten seventy-six from fifty-three was the innings around which Meadowbrook’s entire card was built: early patience, middle-over control, and late acceleration, all while wickets fell often enough around him to prevent the innings from ever becoming comfortable. Leo Bennett’s brief not-out cameo helped the final line reach 154 for six, and that was a serious recovery from where the innings had been. Still, Harborline would have walked off believing they had done a lot right. Victor Hale’s spell was the cleanest of the innings, two wickets for only eighteen and no real release. Ryan Cole took two as well, Adam Pierce removed the opener who had been trying to glue the top together, and Owen Miles stopped Mason before the finish could get completely away. The only frustration was that once Julian had survived the early squeeze, no one could remove him, and that one failure kept Meadowbrook alive. Chasing 155, Harborline opened as if they wanted to end the argument before the surface had time to turn difficult. Blake Porter and Theo Lane struck hard enough to make the target feel suddenly smaller, and the first stand of thirty-six came quickly, full of clean powerplay intent rather than cautious calculation. But Meadowbrook did not fold under that start. Owen Clarke removed Blake, Mason Trent caught and bowled Theo, and Isaac Bell then got Noah King before the end of the fifth over, so Harborline had gone from a flying position to forty-three for three, and that was the moment the chase could have become awkward. The required rate was not yet dangerous, but three wickets down that early in a final changes how every batter reads the next few overs. Victor Hale read them better than anyone. He did not abandon the chase’s tempo, but he stopped the innings from becoming reckless. Aaron Miles was the ideal partner for that repair, positive enough to keep pressure off the set batter and disciplined enough not to turn the stand into a gamble. Their partnership of sixty-eight was the decisive passage of the chase, because it carried Harborline out of the wobble and deep into control. Aaron’s thirty-seven from twenty-seven gave the chase real momentum through the middle, while Victor’s contribution was quieter and more complete: the anchor, the scorer, the decision-maker, the player who made sure the early collapse never became the main story. Mason Trent eventually broke that stand with Meadowbrook still searching for a way back, and Owen Grant then came in and made sure the opening never widened again. His eighteen from eight was short, sharp, and perfectly timed, the sort of finishing hand that compresses a chase before the bowling side can set another trap. By the time Elliot Marsh removed Owen, Harborline were already one hit away from safety. Caleb Finch stayed calm at the end, Victor remained there unbeaten, and the target was crossed in the eighteenth over with five wickets still standing. The difference between the teams was not that Meadowbrook had failed to fight. They fought hard. Julian Brooks played the best rescue innings of the first half, Owen Clarke supported him well, and their bowlers created the early stumble they needed. But Harborline had the fuller match. They had Victor Hale’s new-ball and middle-over control, then his unbeaten fifty-two to complete the chase; they had Aaron Miles to rebuild with him, and Owen Grant to finish before nerves could return. Meadowbrook’s innings depended heavily on one long rescue. Harborline’s chase survived its own wobble because one all-rounder stayed in command of both the situation and the tempo. That is why the five-wicket result feels right. Meadowbrook reached a defendable 154, but Harborline handled the pressure moments more cleanly, and Victor Hale’s combined influence made the player-of-the-match decision straightforward.
