At Windward Cricket Academy Ground in Mariston, Highland Reserve won the toss and chose to field, and for a long stretch that decision looked like one of those choices that keeps offering the bowling side small pieces of encouragement without ever giving them full control, because Meridian A built their innings in two very different movements: first through Arun Vale’s clean top-order hitting, then through the calm late stand between Nolan Reed and Lucas Venn. Rian Cooper and Arun Vale gave Meridian a workable opening, not a runaway one, with Rian nudging the start forward before Kieran Moss bowled him at twenty-four, and that first wicket could have allowed Highland to settle into the innings if Arun had not immediately made the next phase much more expensive. Ethan Lane came in and played the supporting role more than the driving one, seventeen from twenty, a little restrained but useful enough to keep the partnership intact, while Arun became the batter Highland could not fully quiet. He took the shorter ball early, lifted the fuller one when the field came up, and carried Meridian through the first half with fifty-eight from thirty-six, five fours and four sixes, the sort of innings that gives a side momentum even when the scorecard is not completely smooth. The second-wicket stand was worth sixty-two, and by the time Bren Turner finally had Arun caught at eighty-six, Meridian already had a base strong enough to think beyond a merely safe total. Highland did hit back well at that point. Dylan Shaw removed Ethan soon after, Alden Fox trapped Jude Hart in a short, tight spell, and at one hundred and six for four Meridian had lost the clean shape of the innings. That was the danger passage for the batting side. They had used overs, lost the set opener, and still needed someone to turn the last third into something substantial. Nolan Reed did that job with exactly the right tone. He was not as explosive as Arun at the start, and he did not need to be. He stayed long enough to control the tempo after the wobble, then found the right finishing partner in Lucas Venn, whose unbeaten thirty-one from fourteen gave the innings its sharp closing edge. Their unbroken stand of sixty-five was the decisive part of Meridian’s batting effort because it changed the total from something Highland would have felt comfortable chasing into something that demanded a proper innings. Nolan finished unbeaten on forty-seven, Lucas struck three sixes, and the last six overs turned from repair into pressure. Highland’s bowlers had useful moments: Kieran Moss struck first, Bren Turner removed the main opener, Dylan Shaw and Alden Fox both hit during the middle squeeze, and the fielding side did not let Meridian run away completely. But they never found the wicket after one hundred and six for four, and that failure allowed Meridian to close on one hundred and seventy-one for four, a score that looked highly competitive without being out of reach. Highland’s chase began with enough violence to suggest they were not going to be intimidated by the target, Kale Brook striking eighteen from nine and giving Miles Rowan a fast opening breath before Connor Blake removed him. Logan Price then bowled Evan Cross, and when Dane Shaw also fell to Connor with the score at sixty-one, Highland were in that uneasy territory where the chase was not collapsing but was already dependent on one batter staying deep. Miles Rowan was that batter, and his innings is the entire reason the match tilted away from Meridian. He did not begin like a player trying to win the game in one burst. He began by making sure the early damage did not become permanent. First he worked through the Dane Shaw stand, then he built with Owen Parker in the most important stabilizing phase of the chase, a partnership of forty-seven that took Highland to one hundred and eight before Caleb Foster finally bowled Owen. That wicket gave Meridian a real opening because the target was still alive and Highland were four down, but Miles had already shifted from repair into control. Ty Quinn’s twelve was not a large score, but the stand with Miles was worth thirty-seven and came at exactly the right moment, bringing the chase from a slightly awkward middle into the final stretch with the asking rate under control. Ethan Lane’s stumping of Ty at one hundred and forty-five reopened the match briefly, and Logan Price’s wicket of Arden Fox at one hundred and sixty-three gave Meridian one last flicker, but the problem for them was that Miles Rowan never left. He kept taking the right ball on, kept refusing the false shot, and kept adjusting his tempo every time a partner disappeared. His unbeaten ninety-three from fifty-six, with ten fours and four sixes, was not just the biggest innings of the match; it was the innings that connected every broken phase of the chase. Bren Turner arrived for the final act, clipped the ball away, and with Miles still in control Highland reached one hundred and seventy-two for six in the twentieth over’s first half. Meridian had enough bowling to make it uncomfortable: Logan Price and Connor Blake both struck twice, Caleb Foster removed a key middle-order partner, and Ethan Lane briefly turned the endgame tense. But nobody removed Miles Rowan, and in a chase of this size that was the one wicket that mattered above all others. Meridian’s innings had been built well, with Arun Vale’s early fifty and the Nolan-Lucas finish giving them a strong total, but Highland’s reply had the one complete batting hand the match demanded. That is why Highland Reserve won by four wickets, and why Miles Rowan was the clear player of the match. He carried the chase through early damage, middle pressure, late wickets, and the final scoring requirement without ever letting the innings lose its shape.
