At Northfield Park in Riverton, Westhaven chose to bat first and produced one of those innings that never quite looked like a runaway but kept finding enough structure at the right moments to leave Eastmoor chasing more than the surface had seemed to promise. Liam Cross and Mason Vale gave Westhaven the first useful block of the match, and although neither batter looked entirely free from the beginning, they made the new ball phase expensive enough for Eastmoor to feel they had missed a chance. Liam was the sharper striker early, using anything loose enough to cut or drive, and the one clean six in his 32 gave the innings a little early bite. Mason was more patient, less showy, but he was the reason the stand lasted long enough to matter. Their opening partnership reached 62, helped by some extras but still mostly built on sensible batting, and by the time Finley Grant removed Liam, Eastmoor had already spent more than twelve overs trying to force a first opening. Julian Frost then changed the tone of the innings. His spell was the reason Westhaven’s total stayed in the defendable range rather than turning into something much larger. He had Mason stumped after a patient 41, trapped Owen Clarke lbw almost immediately after Owen had tried to counter with a boundary, and later returned to cut through the lower-middle order just when Westhaven were preparing to stretch. The crucial point, though, was that Westhaven never let Julian’s breakthroughs become a full collapse. Noah Turner helped bridge the innings with 30, and once he and Nathan Dale came together, the batting side regained enough control to stop Eastmoor from dictating every over. Nathan Dale’s innings was the most important hand of the match because it sat exactly in the awkward zone between repair and acceleration. He did not walk in with a free platform, and he did not have the luxury of attacking from the first ball. He had to read Julian Frost’s control, Finley Grant’s discipline, and the way Eastmoor were trying to squeeze singles out of the middle overs. First he settled beside Noah, adding 43 and letting the innings move from 102 to 145 without another damaging break. Then he added 44 with Quentin Brooks, and that stand was not glamorous but it was vital because it took Westhaven out of the danger of being held near 180. Quentin’s 10 from 22 was a holding innings, but Nathan used that time well, gradually opening his range, finding gaps square of the wicket, and picking the moment to go over the top rather than forcing it too early. When Julian Frost removed Quentin and Riley Nash in quick succession, Eastmoor again had a route back into the innings, yet Nathan stayed there long enough to build two more small but valuable phases: 20 with Sawyer Holt and then another 10 with Theo Walsh. That is where his 68 mattered most. It was not simply the highest score. It was the innings that kept reconnecting the card every time a wicket threatened to split it apart. Sawyer’s short 9, Theo Walsh’s late 15, Victor Lane’s small contribution, and Wyatt Cole’s final not-out single all helped Westhaven reach 236 all out in the forty-ninth over. Eastmoor’s bowling effort had quality, especially Julian Frost’s 4 for 32 and Finley Grant’s two-wicket control spell, but they paid for the loose edges around those strong phases. Gavin Wells conceded too many wides despite taking a wicket, Harvey Stone and Adrian Cole both had costly patches, and the total extras kept Westhaven moving during overs that should have been tighter. The chase asked Eastmoor for a stable start and one long middle-order hand. They got the second part only halfway. Connor Blake and Adrian Cole began cautiously, but Sawyer Holt removed the captain before the opening stand could become useful. Ethan Price followed soon after, caught off Quentin Brooks, and when Wyatt Cole trapped Adrian Cole lbw, Eastmoor were 34 for 3 with the innings already needing reconstruction rather than simple progression. Dominic Shaw and Benjamin Hart gave them that reconstruction for a while. Their stand of 65 was Eastmoor’s best passage of the match, Benjamin playing the more assertive support hand and Dominic taking the longer view, using patience, soft hands, and the occasional boundary to keep the required work from becoming impossible. For several overs, Eastmoor looked capable of turning the chase into a slow but credible pursuit. Victor Lane broke that shape by removing Benjamin for 31, and that wicket mattered because it pushed Dominic into the role of lone organizer. Dominic then added 35 with Finley Grant, which kept the chase alive on paper, but Noah Turner produced the decisive middle-overs cut when he had Dominic caught and bowled for 47. Two balls later he bowled Isaac Rowe, and Eastmoor’s chase changed completely. From that point, the innings still had runs available, but it no longer had a center. Finley Grant tried to hold, Gavin Wells struck a useful 18, Julian Frost and Harvey Stone added brief resistance, but each partnership was too small to shift the pressure back. Wyatt Cole removed Finley, Theo Walsh took Gavin and Julian, and Quentin Brooks returned to close the innings by removing Harvey Stone, leaving Eastmoor all out for 178. Westhaven’s bowling worked because the wickets were spread across the right moments rather than left to one bowler. Sawyer Holt gave them the first cut, Quentin Brooks removed Ethan early and Harvey at the end, Wyatt Cole broke Adrian and later Finley, Noah Turner produced the most important double incision by taking Dominic and Isaac, Victor Lane removed Benjamin at the peak of Eastmoor’s best stand, and Theo Walsh prevented the tail from dragging the match into a nervy finish. That spread of pressure was the difference between the two sides. Eastmoor had the best individual bowling spell through Julian Frost, but Westhaven had the more complete match structure. Their first innings had an opening base, a middle repair, Nathan Dale’s anchoring hand, and enough tail-end additions to push the score beyond easy reach. Eastmoor’s reply had one promising stand and one half-recovery, but it never had the repeated rebuilding that Westhaven found. Nathan Dale was therefore the correct player of the match: his 68 did not decorate an already dominant innings, it kept Westhaven upright through the section where the match could have turned, and once that total was on the board, the bowling unit had enough pressure to make 236 feel much bigger than it looked at first glance.
