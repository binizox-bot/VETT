Good afternoon from Willowfield Ground in Eastmoor, where Ridgebank XI chose to bat first and almost immediately found themselves dragged into the kind of innings that feels less like construction and more like emergency repair, because Kieran Doyle’s new-ball spell did not merely give Ashford XI a tidy start, it tore the top of the card open before Ridgebank had any chance to define their own tempo. Rian Wells went for nothing, caught behind before the innings had a pulse, Gavin Rowe tried to punch back with three early boundaries and briefly gave the batting side a flash of momentum, but Doyle removed him as well, and when Bren Shaw later fell to the same bowler after a cramped stay, Ridgebank were thirty-seven for three and already batting from underneath the game. Dorian Ward and Luca Price gave the first real attempt at order, not spectacularly, not with the kind of fluent stand that changes a fielding captain’s plans, but with enough composure to stop the innings from folding completely. Dorian’s twenty-four had useful shape and a few well-timed strokes, while Luca’s slower twenty-three was the more awkward but necessary hand, the kind of innings that looks heavy on the card and still matters because someone has to stand in the mess and make the next over exist. Jordan Pike removed Dorian just as that pair had begun to make the score look repairable, and Ridgebank had to start again. Finn Cross then produced a brief middle-overs push, sixteen from eleven with a clean six, and for a moment the innings looked as if it might gather itself in two stages, only for Harvey Stone to cut that off and leave Ridgebank five down before the thirteenth over was done. The real damage came soon after, when Luca Price was run out and Hayden Marsh followed immediately without adding a run to the partnership. That two-ball sequence at one hundred changed the whole emotional weight of the innings. A side that might still have imagined a late push toward something in the high one-fifties was suddenly seven down and looking at the possibility of finishing far too light. Landon Barrett was the player who prevented that. His unbeaten forty from twenty-five was not just a lower-order cameo; it was the innings that gave Ridgebank something real to defend. At first he had to steady things with Mason Clarke, then after Isaac Bell trapped Mason in front, Landon took command of the final stretch with Kyle Mercer surviving beside him. The last stand of thirty-six was the most important batting passage Ridgebank produced, not because Kyle made many himself, but because he stayed there while Landon struck cleanly, found the rope, cleared it three times, and dragged the total from a damaged one hundred and eight for eight to one hundred and forty-four for eight. Without that finish the match is almost gone at the break. With it, Ashford still had work to do. Their bowling card reflected the way they had controlled most of the innings: Doyle’s three wickets created the first collapse, Isaac Bell and Harvey Stone kept the middle contained, Jordan Pike removed the captain, and even though Elliot Dean and Grant Bishop leaked at the wrong times, Ashford had still forced Ridgebank into a total built more by rescue than by command. Chasing one hundred and forty-five, Ashford did not get the clean, simple start the target might have invited. Harvey Stone made ten quickly enough to give the reply a little early movement, but Gavin Rowe had him caught by Luca Price, then removed Calvin Drake after a slow stay, and suddenly Ashford were seventeen for two with the chase already requiring someone to bat through rather than simply swing through. Jonah Vale became that player. His unbeaten sixty-eight from fifty was the complete innings of the match because it never lived in one mood. Early on he absorbed the pressure, letting Damian Frost take enough of the risk in a third-wicket stand that restored the chase from danger to structure. Damian’s twenty-eight from twenty-five was valuable precisely because it was not reckless; he stayed long enough, hit cleanly enough, and allowed Jonah to build without having to force every scoring moment himself. Gavin Rowe’s third wicket, Damian caught by Rian Wells, kept Ridgebank alive, and when Landon Barrett struck back with his own caught-and-bowled dismissal of Elliot Dean after a useful little thirteen, the chase still had enough tension to be real. Adrian Cole’s run-out added another twist, and at one hundred and five for five Ridgebank could still imagine the match tightening if Jonah made one mistake. He did not. Mason Hale came in and gave the next little burst, a nine from seven that included a six and helped move Ashford to within sight of the line before Landon trapped him lbw. That wicket at one hundred and twenty-eight for six was Ridgebank’s last proper opening. Grant Bishop then arrived and played the ideal finishing hand, unbeaten on eight from four, sharp enough to prevent the pressure from settling entirely back onto Jonah. The final stand of seventeen was small in raw size but decisive in match feel: Jonah still there, Grant finding the useful boundary, the target shrinking without panic. Ashford reached one hundred and forty-five for six in the twentieth over and won by four wickets, and the result made sense because they had the one batter who controlled the chase from the inside. Ridgebank had fought hard. Gavin Rowe’s three wickets made the defense far more serious than the target suggested, Landon Barrett’s two wickets after his rescue innings kept the game alive, Mason Clarke bowled a tight opening spell, and the fielding pressure produced one important run-out. But they never removed Jonah Vale, and that was the difference. Ridgebank’s first innings had been defined by recovery after damage; Ashford’s chase was defined by continuity through damage. Kieran Doyle set up the match with the ball, Landon Barrett nearly changed it with a late rescue and middle-overs wickets, yet Jonah Vale owned the decisive thread from the second over to the winning runs. That is why Ashford won, and why his player-of-the-match award fits the rebuilt scorecard cleanly.
