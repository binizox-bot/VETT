At Harborline Training Oval in Northport, Seabrook A chose to bat first and built the kind of total that never quite looked outrageous while it was being assembled, yet kept becoming harder to chase once each phase was added on top of the previous one, because the innings was not one long burst of hitting, it was a chain of useful answers. Jonah Ward and Owen Price gave them the first one. The opening stand was worth forty-two, and although it did not feel like a pure powerplay demolition, it was clean enough to stop Meridian A from settling into the early squeeze they clearly wanted. Jonah played the steadier hand, getting to twenty-four from twenty balls, while Owen was sharper from the other end, using the harder ball better and punishing the fuller length when it arrived. Luke Barrett eventually removed Jonah, and that wicket mattered because Luke was the one Meridian bowler who gave the first innings real control, but the start had already done enough to place Seabrook in a usable position. Owen and Liam Stone then added another small layer, not a stand that changed the match on its own, just enough to keep the innings from breaking when the first wicket fell. Owen’s thirty-eight from twenty-three had the more obvious tempo, four boundaries and two clean swings over the rope, and when Liam Foster finally had him caught at seventy for two, Meridian could feel they had dragged the innings into a more manageable middle. Liam Stone stayed around for a while, and Caleb Hayes joined him, but the third stand was more of a holding passage than a launch. Tyler Brooks removed Liam at eighty-five, and at that point the innings was still open to interpretation: Seabrook had a base, but not yet a score that looked obviously above par. The match turned toward Seabrook in the next partnership. Connor Vale and Caleb Hayes gave the innings its real spine with a stand of sixty-four, and the contrast between them worked almost perfectly. Caleb played the more connecting role, thirty from twenty-three, keeping the board moving without demanding the spotlight. Connor Vale supplied the lift, thirty-nine from twenty-seven, three fours, two sixes, and the kind of late-middle overs hand that changes how a fielding side feels about the last five overs. Meridian had done a lot right before that. Caleb Shaw’s four overs were tidy. Luke Barrett had controlled his spell beautifully. But once Connor and Caleb settled, the innings began to lean forward again, and Seabrook moved from eighty-five for three to one hundred and forty-nine before the next wicket. Liam Foster did get Connor at that point, and Evan Reed struck twice in the final over, removing Ryan Cole and then Finn Mercer, but those wickets came after the important scoring work had been done. Caleb Hayes was run out on the penultimate ball, Dylan Shaw stayed unbeaten with a small late contribution, and Seabrook finished on one hundred and sixty-eight for seven. The total felt properly competitive because it had been assembled without relying on one batter alone: Jonah and Owen set it up, Liam kept one middle passage alive, Caleb and Connor gave it weight, and the tiny late pieces pushed it past the line where Meridian could chase without pressure. Meridian’s bowlers had individual wins, especially Luke Barrett’s economical spell and Liam Foster’s two wickets, but they never kept Seabrook quiet for long enough after the twelfth over, and that is why the score moved into the high one-sixties rather than stopping around the mid-one-fifties. Meridian’s reply began as though they had every intention of making that target look smaller. Aaron Wells came out swinging hard, and his twenty-six from eleven balls gave the chase immediate danger. Tyler Finch was not quite as clean at first, but his three sixes made it clear that he was capable of turning any over into a problem. Their opening stand of forty-four came quickly, and even after Zane Fox removed Aaron, Meridian still had the chase moving at a rate that kept Seabrook under pressure. Then came the unusual hinge of the innings. Tyler Finch, on thirty-one, retired hurt after the next passage, and the scoreboard still looked healthy enough, but the chase had lost one of its two original engines. That changed the feel of everything that followed. Isaac Bennett and Connor Wells tried to rebuild the tempo rather than just survive, yet Evan Turner removed Isaac at seventy-five, and from there Seabrook began cutting into the chase at exactly the points Meridian needed continuity. Leo Turner arrived with the captain’s responsibility and struck one clean six, but Finn Mercer got him before the stand could become meaningful. Mason Hart followed to the same bowler. Connor Wells, who had been trying to hold the shape together after Tyler left, was caught off Ryan Cole, and suddenly Meridian had gone from a dangerous opening to a chase being restarted every couple of overs. Luke Barrett could not give the innings the long support it needed and fell to Aiden Moss. Caleb Shaw came in at a point where Ryan Foster was beginning to take more responsibility, but Finn Mercer struck again, this time with Owen Price completing the stumping. That wicket was the one that fully tilted the last phase toward Seabrook, because it removed the possibility of one settled lower-order stand forming around Ryan. Ryan Foster fought well. His unbeaten thirty-four from twenty-five was not empty hitting; he genuinely kept Meridian alive longer than they had any right to be after the middle-order slide. He cleared the rope three times, found enough strike to make Seabrook keep thinking, and with Liam Foster took the final stand through to the end. But the chase had already lost too much shape. The openers had created a proper launch, Tyler’s retirement had taken away the next layer, and the middle order had been cut down before Ryan could turn his counterattack into a real finish. Finn Mercer’s spell sits at the center of that defensive win. He made nothing with the bat on the last ball of Seabrook’s innings, but with the ball he removed the captain, then Mason Hart, then Caleb Shaw, and those wickets were not decorative. They were the wickets that stopped Meridian from building around their promising start. Evan Turner, Ryan Cole, Zane Fox, and Aiden Moss all gave Seabrook important supporting breakthroughs, but Finn’s three for twenty-three was the spell that made the chase feel increasingly fragmented. That is why the final score of one hundred and forty-seven for seven tells the story properly. Meridian were not blown away early. They were dangerous early. They had Aaron’s burst, Tyler’s power, and Ryan Foster’s late resistance. What they did not have was a stable middle. Seabrook, by contrast, had a more complete structure: a solid opening stand, a strong Connor-Caleb partnership, and then a bowling unit that kept attacking the next new batter before the chase could reset. The result, a twenty-one-run win, came from that difference in continuity. Meridian had the louder first few overs of the chase, but Seabrook had the better full innings and the sharper pressure moments, and Finn Mercer earned player of the match because his spell was the point where a threatening pursuit became a broken one.
