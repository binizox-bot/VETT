At Lakeside Oval in Eastport, Granite XI won the toss, chose to field, and for the first stretch that decision looked more than defensible, because Eastshore were never allowed to turn the early overs into a clean batting parade, even though Aaron Vale and Blake Rowan did enough to make the first phase useful rather than timid. Aaron was the steadier hand, waiting on the fuller ball and taking his boundaries when Cameron Stone or Brandon Shaw overpitched, while Blake gave the stand a sharper edge, especially when he got width and could throw his hands through the off side. Their opening partnership moved Eastshore into a workable position, yet it never felt completely out of Granite’s reach because Cameron Stone kept coming back to the right area and eventually found the first break by bowling Aaron. Once Blake Rowan followed, caught off the same bowler after giving the innings its early push, the tone changed. Carter Holt did not collapse the innings, but he could not free it either, and when Dominic Hale had him stumped, Eastshore were three down with the innings still not fully formed. That was the exact point where the match needed an innings of control rather than noise, and Dylan Archer supplied it. His 64 was not the fastest innings of the day and not the most violent, but it was the one that made every later Eastshore contribution possible. He started by simply refusing to let Granite turn three wickets into a complete squeeze, playing straight, taking the easy single, and making the bowlers work through longer spells without the reward of another sudden mistake. Evan Marsh then became the partner who let that control turn into a real platform. Evan did not need to dominate. His 44 from 60 carried the right kind of restraint, enough rotation to keep the partnership alive, enough discipline to survive Granite’s tighter overs, and enough scoring sense to stop Dylan from having to manufacture every shift in momentum alone. Their stand was the spine of the innings, lifting Eastshore from a fragile middle point into a position of authority. What made it damaging was not just the runs, it was the time it consumed and the pressure it reversed. Granite had bowlers doing decent work: Cameron had already made two breakthroughs, Dominic was keeping the batters honest, and Finley Cross was beginning to find the sort of rhythm that would later bring wickets. Still, through the long Dylan-and-Evan phase, those efforts became containment rather than control. Dylan found boundaries at important moments, especially when the innings had gone quiet for a few overs, and Evan kept the gaps alive so the field never fully came in. When Finley Cross finally bowled Dylan, Eastshore had already moved into a much safer part of the game. Granite then threatened to drag the score down again. Julian Brooks went quickly, Evan followed soon after, and Felix Grant’s short stay ended before he could turn his boundary into a proper cameo. But the lower order did the one thing required after a long middle-overs rebuild: it did not waste the platform. Gavin Price stayed calm at one end, giving the final overs a fixed point, while Hudson Lane added the one burst of clean late hitting that changed the finish from merely respectable to difficult. His two straight hits over the rope forced Granite to defend deeper, and even after Finley Cross removed him to claim his fourth wicket, Eastshore squeezed enough from Gavin Price, Ian Foster, and the final ball to close at 243 for 9. It was not an overwhelming first-innings number in isolation, but the way it was built made it feel durable: an opening base, a three-wicket wobble, a long repair through Dylan and Evan, then a late push from Gavin and Hudson. Granite’s bowling had strong parts, particularly Cameron Stone’s early pair and Finley Cross’s four wickets through the middle and end, but they never made wickets contagious for long enough. The chase then stripped away any uncertainty about whether 243 was enough. Hayden Marsh was bowled by Gavin Price almost immediately, and that first wicket set the wrong mood for Granite XI because it meant Gavin Price, the only top-order batter who looked capable of building something, had to start repairing before the innings had even opened its shoulders. Finley Cross stayed for a while without truly moving the score, then Logan West found him with Carter Holt taking the catch, and the chase was already entering a low, awkward rhythm. Gavin Price did fight. His 20 was the one early innings with shape, and for a few overs beside Cameron Stone he made Granite look as though they might at least force Eastshore to work through a second spell. Julian Brooks removed him, and then bowled Isaac Cole almost immediately afterward, and that short sequence broke the innings more deeply than the numbers alone suggest. Cameron Stone went soon after to Logan West, and at that point Granite were five down with barely anything established beyond Gavin’s brief resistance. Brandon Shaw and Jordan Wells produced the only partnership that looked like a genuine attempt to stop the fall. Brandon was careful, Jordan even more so, and together they carried Granite through the one passage where the innings stopped bleeding. But it was survival rather than recovery, and that distinction mattered. The required score had already become irrelevant; the real question was whether Granite could build a phase that looked stable. Evan Marsh ended Brandon’s resistance, and once Hudson Lane came on, the lower order had no room left. Jordan Wells fell behind the wicket, Elliot Dean was trapped before he could settle, and Kyle Mercer’s short stay ended with Evan Marsh under the catch. Dominic Hale remained not out and gave the card a little late resistance, but Gavin Price returned to finish the innings by trapping Adrian Wells, leaving Granite XI all out for 73 in the twenty-fifth over. Eastshore’s bowling was complete because it came in different forms. Gavin Price gave them the first wicket and the last. Logan West strangled the early-middle overs with control and two wickets. Julian Brooks delivered the decisive double cut around Gavin Price and Isaac Cole. Evan Marsh broke the only partnership that had begun to resemble resistance. Hudson Lane finished the lower order with three wickets and made sure the innings did not drift toward a more respectable total. That is why the final margin was so large. Eastshore did not win through one freak spell or one isolated batting performance. They won because every time the match required a new layer, someone supplied it. Aaron and Blake gave the start. Dylan Archer and Evan Marsh rebuilt the innings. Gavin Price and Hudson Lane made the finish useful. Then the bowlers attacked Granite from the first over and never allowed a real second phase. Dylan Archer was the correct Player of the Match because his 64 sat at the exact hinge of the contest: without that innings, Eastshore’s early wobble could have become a low, chaseable total; with it, the later hitters had a platform, and the bowlers had more than enough room to turn pressure into a crushing win.
