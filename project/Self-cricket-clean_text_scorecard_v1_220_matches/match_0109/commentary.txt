At Pinecrest Oval in Westford, Estuary Women won the toss and chose to bat, but the innings they produced never quite felt like something they owned. It began in the strangest possible way, with Ridgewood Women spraying enough early wides to make the scoreboard move before the bat had done any meaningful work, and yet even inside that looseness the batting side looked unsettled. Sana Kapoor was run out almost immediately after a hesitant call, Jade Hla’s throw turning a gift-fed start into a wicket, and that dismissal captured the whole strange texture of the innings: Estuary were receiving help, sometimes plenty of it, but they were not turning that help into control. Nera Patel and Leena Varma then tried to give the innings a shape, and for a while they did just enough to move it out of complete early disorder. Nera was never fluent, mostly blocking, nudging, trying to survive the line rather than dictate it, while Leena looked like the one batter prepared to accept the ugly work of staying there. Their stand was useful, but it was also revealing, because a large chunk of it came through wides rather than clean scoring. Ridgewood were loose enough to keep Estuary breathing, but accurate enough in the moments that mattered to prevent any real acceleration. Tara Lin finally broke that partnership when Nera was caught by Mira Cho, and from there the innings depended almost entirely on Leena holding a thin line through the middle. Kavita Sen gave her the best proper support Estuary found, not explosive, not dominant, but composed enough to help create a second useful passage. Noor Thein’s spell was important in that section because she did not chase wickets recklessly; she kept the pace controlled, denied easy release, and forced Kavita to manufacture something. When Kavita was stumped by Rina Moon, the dismissal felt like the natural result of a batter trying to push against a scoreboard that had never moved naturally. Leena was still there, still trying to carry the innings through to the last five overs, but Ridgewood’s fielding then cut out the spine of the card. Rina Moon’s work in the field ended Leena’s stay, and once the anchor had gone, Estuary’s innings shifted from modest construction into late survival. Mira Nair came and went against Amina Kade, Reva Das was bowled by the same bowler soon after, and Amina’s spell became the point where Ridgewood turned pressure into damage. Sonia Malik, to her credit, kept the final overs from collapsing completely. She did not take the innings anywhere dramatic, but she stayed, picked up what was available, and let Tara Iqbal add a small passage of resistance before Amina struck again. Lina Hassan’s final-over run out, with Amina involved once more, left Estuary on ninety-three for eight, a total that looked higher than the batting alone deserved because the extras column had done so much of the carrying. Twenty-eight extras in a twenty-over innings is a major feature, not a footnote, and Ridgewood would know they had been too generous. At the same time, Estuary would know they had failed to punish that generosity. Only Leena made a score of real substance, Kavita and Sonia offered smaller pieces, and the rest of the card never found enough command to make the innings feel truly defendable. Ridgewood’s bowling was therefore mixed in discipline but strong in control points. Amina supplied the cutting edge with three wickets and a run-out involvement, Noor kept the middle overs tight, Tara found the first proper breakthrough after the opening run-out, and the fielding repeatedly turned half-pressure into wickets. The chase of ninety-four should have been manageable, but it was not completely clean. Tara Lin fell early, and Sonia Malik’s first wicket gave Estuary the small opening they needed if they were going to make a low defence awkward. Noor Thein joined Mira Cho and briefly settled the reply, yet Leena Varma struck to remove the Ridgewood captain before the chase could become too comfortable. That left Ridgewood two down with the target still requiring proper work, and this is where Mira’s innings mattered. She was the most natural-looking batter in the chase, not because she played wildly, but because she seemed to know which balls deserved a scoring shot and which ones only needed to be waited out. Her boundaries came when Estuary missed length, and between those moments she kept the chase moving just enough that the wickets did not create panic. Amina Kade then became the stabilizing presence beside her. After already shaping the first innings with the ball, Amina’s batting had a different tone: less headline, more management. She gave Mira enough support to build the decisive stand, kept the scoreboard from freezing, and made sure Ridgewood did not turn a small target into a nervous one. Maya Fern eventually removed Mira lbw, and that wicket gave Estuary one more chance to ask questions. Sela Htun then fell stumped, also to Maya, and suddenly the chase had a little roughness around it. It was not a crisis, but it was no longer a perfectly smooth walk either. Amina stayed long enough to take the equation down to almost nothing, and even when Sonia Malik bowled her with the score nearly level, the match had already moved beyond Estuary’s reach. Rina Moon remained composed, Kiri Dawn came in and ended it quickly with one clean boundary, and Ridgewood crossed the line in the sixteenth over with five wickets still standing. The match reads best as a contest between two imperfect sides, with Ridgewood handling the disorder better. Estuary’s innings was inflated by wides and byes, held together mainly by Leena, and repeatedly cut by fielding mistakes under pressure. Ridgewood’s chase lost more wickets than it needed to, but Mira gave it fluency, Amina gave it all-round control, and the lower middle finished before the target could become awkward. Amina Kade as Player of the Match fits the structure of the game because she influenced both decisive halves: she broke Estuary’s lower-middle resistance with the ball, stayed involved in the field, and then made the chase safe when a couple of wickets could have made it untidy. By the end, Ridgewood had not played a spotless match, but they had played the more functional one. They leaked runs without losing the plot, chased with interruptions without losing the match, and turned a modest target into a five-wicket win through better control of the key moments.
