At Riverside Oval in the East Midlands, Hillcrest chose to bat first and spent almost the entire innings trying to turn Cole Barrett’s long presence into something large enough to defend, because the card around him kept moving in short, useful, but incomplete bursts, never quite collapsing, never quite gathering the second major hand that would have made the total feel heavy. The start had some purpose. Mason Ward came out quickly, striking two early boundaries and giving Hillcrest the sharp little lift a batting side wants after choosing to set the target, while Barrett took the more measured role from the other end, watching the pace, judging which length was skidding on, and refusing to let the first two overs become reckless. That opening stand reached twenty-two before Elias Fox removed Mason, and although it was not a huge partnership, it did at least put Hillcrest into the innings with something on the board. The problem was what happened after that: every time Barrett seemed ready to receive proper support, Lakeside found the interruption. Nolan Hayes stayed for a while without ever taking control, adding twenty-five with Barrett but using enough balls that the innings began to feel heavier than the score itself. Aaron Mills broke that stand, and then Theo Grant briefly sharpened the tempo with a run-a-ball-plus cameo, only for Lucas Stone to cut him off at sixty-seven. The next wicket came almost immediately, Isaac Reed bowled before he could settle, and suddenly Hillcrest were four down with Barrett still there but surrounded by a card that had not produced a single partnership big enough to change the match. That is what made the fifth-wicket stand with Owen Carter so important. Carter’s thirteen from thirteen was not explosive, but it gave Barrett a small window to breathe, and the pair added thirty, the best stand of Hillcrest’s innings. Barrett used that period to move from survival into control, taking the single early in the over, picking off anything drifting onto the pads, and then saving the bigger shot for the ball that actually deserved it. Owen eventually fell to Daniel Shaw, and Ryan Wells followed after a small useful stay, but Barrett kept stretching the innings forward, never letting Lakeside bowl at a completely exposed tail. Ben Archer’s brief four helped, Liam Brooks survived the close, and Barrett finished unbeaten on sixty-eight from fifty-six, a proper carrying hand in an innings that needed him almost from the third over onward. Hillcrest’s final 136 for seven was therefore respectable without being secure. It had one durable innings, several little pieces of support, and twelve extras, but it lacked the one second batter who could have turned a working score into a threatening one. Lakeside’s bowlers did their job by refusing to let any partner stay too long. Aaron Mills’ two wickets through the upper middle kept the innings from settling, Elias Fox took the first bite, Lucas Stone removed Theo before he could kick on, Daniel Shaw and Victor Hale clipped away the middle, and Caleb Frost got Ben Archer just as Hillcrest were trying to steal a final push. The chase began with Hillcrest getting exactly the brief opening they needed. Caleb Stone and Aaron Wells had already moved Lakeside to twenty-four when Isaac Reed had Caleb caught, and then Marcus Bell flashed one boundary before falling two balls later, also to Isaac. At twenty-eight for two, Hillcrest had a flicker of real pressure: the target was not large, but the chase was no longer spotless, and another wicket there might have dragged the match toward the kind of awkward, low-target scramble that defending sides dream about. Aaron Wells did not allow that possibility to live. He had already looked clean in the first stand, and once Jordan Marsh joined him, the whole rhythm of the innings changed. Wells stopped treating the chase like something to be negotiated and began treating it like something to be ended. He drove firmly through the infield, picked up length early, and punished anything short with the confidence of a batter who knew the required rate had stopped mattering the moment the bowlers lost their line. Marsh was even more violent from the other end. His unbeaten forty-seven from eighteen balls gave the chase a second blade, and that is what broke Hillcrest completely. If Wells had been attacking alone, they might still have tried to protect one side of the field and force him into risk. With Marsh clearing the rope repeatedly, that option disappeared. The unbeaten third-wicket stand of one hundred and nine came in a little under seven overs, and it made Hillcrest’s twenty overs of careful accumulation look suddenly too small for the conditions. Ben Archer and Owen Carter could not hold the new ball tightly enough. Isaac Reed had the only two wickets but was soon dragged into the same scoring storm as everyone else. Ryan Wells and Victor Lane tried to change pace, but by then Lakeside were no longer chasing in phases; they were racing through a target already stripped of danger. Wells finished unbeaten on seventy-one from thirty-one, Marsh unbeaten on forty-seven from eighteen, and Lakeside crossed the line at 137 for two in 10.1 overs. The result looks brutal because it was brutal after the third wicket partnership began. Hillcrest had one excellent anchor in Cole Barrett, but Lakeside had the innings that actually decided the match: Aaron Wells absorbing the early double strike, then turning the chase into a short, clean assault with Marsh beside him. That is why Wells was the clear player of the match. Barrett gave Hillcrest a total. Wells made that total feel temporary. Once Lakeside recovered from twenty-eight for two, the match stopped being a chase and became a countdown, and the eight-wicket margin reflects the difference between a side that survived its innings and a side that imposed itself on the reply.
