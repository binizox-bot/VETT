At Lakeside Cricket Ground in Eastport, with Southport choosing to field in a final that never really felt ordinary from the first over, Ridgefield built the sort of innings that looked commanding on the final card but was actually shaped by three very different passages: the opening stand that gave them control, the middle wobble that briefly brought Southport back into the match, and then Marcus Ward’s huge intervention with Graham Holt that turned a strong score into a final-winning one. Adrian Cole and Brandon Vale started with the calm aggression of a pair who understood that batting first in a final does not require panic, only control of tempo. Adrian was the cleaner striker early, strong whenever Connor Hale or Aiden Ford offered him width and quick to punish anything overpitched through the off side, while Brandon’s hand had a slightly heavier rhythm, not always fluent ball by ball but dangerous whenever he got length to swing through. The opening stand reached 116, and although Southport gave away a handful of extras, the damage was not built on charity. It was built on two batters making the field chase the game before the first wicket had even arrived. Connor Hale eventually removed Adrian for 54, Felix Cross taking the catch, and that was Southport’s first real breath of the innings because Adrian had been the batter keeping the early flow clean. Brandon stayed a little longer with Cameron Stone, but Owen Clarke’s run-out ended his 60 and changed the mood of the innings from steady authority into a more complicated middle phase. Cameron Stone did his job well, making 38 from 38, and the 48 he added with Marcus Ward kept Ridgefield ahead of the match, but Logan Reed’s return brought Southport a proper opening. Cameron was caught and bowled, Elliott Hayes came and went cheaply, and when Caleb Frost pinned Felix Cross lbw, Ridgefield had slipped from a position of total comfort into 203 for 5. That was the moment when Southport had a real chance to pull the final back toward something manageable. One more wicket there and the innings might have been contained below a very different kind of target. Marcus Ward made sure that chance vanished. At first his innings had been controlled, almost watchful, the kind of hand designed to keep the innings from shaking after wickets. Then, once Graham Holt settled beside him, Marcus changed the whole scale of the game. Graham’s 32 from 42 was not the headline performance, yet it was one of the most important innings in the match because it gave Marcus a stable partner at exactly the point where Southport wanted to isolate him. Graham ran hard, held one end, found a couple of scoring shots when the field started leaning too heavily toward Marcus, and made sure the sixth-wicket stand became a true partnership rather than one man dragging the innings alone. Marcus then did the rest. His 103 from 74 was the innings that broke Southport’s fielding plan apart. Twelve fours, four sixes, and more importantly, perfect timing of aggression. When the bowlers went defensive, he found the single early and the boundary late. When they missed full, he drove straight. When they dragged short, he was ready to hit square or over midwicket. The stand with Graham reached 100, and that was the decisive passage of the first innings because it took Ridgefield from a good but vulnerable 203 for 5 to 303 for 6. Dylan Grant eventually removed Marcus, and soon after Graham also went, but the match had already moved. Hayden Brooks added a brief 11, Isaac Lane stayed unbeaten with 9, Jordan Dale helped finish the last over, and Ridgefield closed on 337 for 8. Southport’s bowling had its moments. Logan Reed’s two wickets created the middle squeeze. Caleb Frost’s spell was the most disciplined of the main options. Dylan Grant dismissed Marcus and Graham, which gave the figures late respectability. Connor Hale got the first breakthrough. Aiden Ford closed off Hayden. Yet Southport never managed to own two phases in a row. Every time they got a wicket, Ridgefield found another structure, and every time they seemed close to control, Marcus Ward changed the cost of the next over. Chasing 338, Southport needed a start that would make the target feel like a sequence rather than a mountain, and for the first nine overs they gave themselves exactly that. Ethan Moore and Liam Hughes came out with intent, Ethan especially sharp through the off side, getting to 31 from 30 and putting early pressure back onto Kyle Mercer and Elliott Hayes. Liam Hughes started a little more measured, then grew into the chase, and with seven extras folded into that opening stand, Southport reached 66 before Ridgefield made the first cut. Kyle Mercer removed Ethan, and although Noah Reed helped Liam push the score along briefly, Isaac Lane’s lbw against Noah stopped that second stand before it could become meaningful. The chase was still alive, though, because Liam Hughes and Owen Clarke then produced Southport’s best passage of the innings. Liam’s 58 from 50 was the clearest batting answer Southport had, five fours and a six, enough pace to keep the scoreboard honest and enough control to prevent Ridgefield from treating the chase as a formality. Owen Clarke’s 43 from 45 was the right supporting innings too, calm without being dead, busy enough to keep the stand moving, and by the time the pair carried Southport to 139, the final still had a heartbeat. Jordan Dale changed that. His wicket of Liam Hughes was the central blow of the chase, because Liam was the one batter who had matched the size of the target with the right tempo. Caleb Frost and Owen Clarke added 24, but Graham Holt removed Caleb, and when Jordan Dale also got Owen Clarke, Southport’s chase lost its second stabilizer. From 163 for 4 to 165 for 5, the innings moved from possible chase into pressure management, and Ridgefield sensed it immediately. Mason Stone and Logan Reed tried to patch something together, Mason making 11 and Logan 16, but Hayden Brooks broke both pieces before they could become more than small resistance. Aiden Ford briefly gave the reply a spark with two sixes in his 12, but Jordan Dale removed him too, and that was the last moment where Southport looked capable of pushing back with any violence. Connor Hale stayed around, but his unbeaten 5 from 25 was survival rather than threat, and once Kyle Mercer bowled Dylan Grant and Isaac Lane finished Parker Lane, Southport were all out for 222 in 41.2 overs. The chase had not been a simple collapse from the first ball. It had a real opening stand, a genuine Liam-Owen rebuilding phase, and a few lower-order flickers. What it never had was continuity after the third wicket. Ridgefield’s bowling worked because the wickets were placed almost perfectly across the innings. Kyle Mercer broke the opening and later cut off Dylan Grant. Isaac Lane took Noah Reed and the final wicket. Graham Holt removed Caleb Frost at a point where Southport still needed a middle-order platform. Hayden Brooks took Mason Stone and Logan Reed, which stopped the lower middle from creating another phase. Jordan Dale was the most decisive in the chase’s central corridor, removing Liam Hughes, Owen Clarke, and Aiden Ford, the three batters most capable of changing either the rhythm or the emotional direction of the pursuit. That is why the final margin of 115 runs feels accurate rather than inflated. Ridgefield were not merely bigger with the bat. They were better at connecting phases. Adrian Cole and Brandon Vale gave them the start. Cameron Stone bridged the first transition. Marcus Ward rescued and then dominated the vulnerable middle. Graham Holt gave the hundred-maker the support that made the innings explode. The tail added just enough to keep the total climbing through the full fifty. Southport, by contrast, had two good phases in the chase but never a full innings. Ethan Moore and Liam Hughes gave them belief, Liam and Owen Clarke briefly gave them structure, but after that every possible recovery was cut away before it had time to become serious. Marcus Ward was the right player of the match because his hundred did not sit on top of a risk-free platform. It came after Ridgefield had been dragged into genuine danger at five down, and it turned that danger into the defining stand of the final. Everything that followed, including Southport’s chase starting well and then fading under the weight of the target, was downstream of that innings.
