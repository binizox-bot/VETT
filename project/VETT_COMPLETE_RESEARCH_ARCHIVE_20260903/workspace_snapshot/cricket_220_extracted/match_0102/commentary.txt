Greenfield Park in the East Midlands had that slightly false calm at the start, the kind of afternoon where a captain can win the toss, choose to bat, and still discover within a few overs that the match has already shifted into damage control, and that was exactly what happened to Ridgefield, because Eastport’s new-ball pair struck early enough and accurately enough to make the innings feel broken before it had even reached its first real decision point. Noah Carter went almost immediately, pushing at Ryan Cole and giving Owen Hart the catch before the batting side had a run from the bat, and Mason Blake’s run-out shortly after made the opening damage feel even worse because it was not just good bowling now, it was pressure turning into poor running. Ethan Ward tried to stitch the start together with a few controlled strokes, but Ryan Cole returned to remove him as well, and when Caleb Moore was bowled by Isaac Bell, Ridgefield were twenty-three for four and already searching for rescue rather than platform. That early passage is the important foundation of the match, because every later Ridgefield run had to be understood against it. They were never building freely. They were trying to make a damaged innings respectable. Daniel Price and Oliver Grant did the first part of that work, adding twenty-seven and at least giving the innings a middle, Oliver striking one clean six and Daniel playing the steadier hand, but Isaac Bell broke that stand before it could become a real turn, and Ridgefield were still only halfway to safety when Jacob Ellis arrived as the one batter who finally changed the tone. Jacob’s forty-one from thirty-one was the only innings in the card that looked capable of taking Eastport out of their comfortable rhythm. He came in after the fifth wicket, hit with clearer intent than anyone before him, and with Daniel first, then Henry Brooks, then Logan Hayes, he kept dragging the total into a range that Eastport would at least have to chase properly. His stand with Daniel was worth twenty-two, then another small one with Henry kept the innings from folding, and the partnership with Logan was the one that made 118 possible rather than something down near a hundred. Felix Stone’s spell through this phase was decisive, because he did not simply collect tail-end wickets after the damage was done. He removed Daniel Price, then Henry Brooks, then Jacob Ellis, and each wicket arrived at a point when Ridgefield were trying to stretch the recovery into something more useful. That is why his three for eighteen mattered so much. It cut the innings off before Jacob could fully cash in. Logan Hayes and the last pair did squeeze out a few more, helped by late extras, and Sam Allen’s wicket on 118 closed the card with one ball still unused in the nineteenth over, but the innings never escaped its original wound. Eastport had distributed the work well: Ryan Cole struck twice at the top, Isaac Bell stopped the first repair, Felix controlled the most important middle-lower phase, and Nathan King cleaned up the final pair. Ridgefield’s 118 was not a surrender, because Jacob had given it some resistance and the tail had fought for scraps, but it was still a total built from a collapse, not from a plan. Eastport’s chase then began with enough trouble to keep the game honest. Leo Grant fell almost immediately, caught behind off Peter Shaw, and Paul Jackson followed soon after, the same bowler finding the edge again, so at sixteen for two the target briefly looked heavier than it had at the interval. That is where Felix Stone completed the second half of his match. He did not treat the chase as if the low target gave him permission to be careless. He first rebuilt with Mark Harris, who made eighteen from sixteen and gave Eastport the brisk support they needed after two early wickets, then carried the innings into control with Brian Martin. The stand with Mark was worth thirty-four and moved Eastport out of the opening wobble. The stand with Brian was even more important because it took the score to ninety-five and turned the chase from a contest into a managed finish. Brian’s twenty-one was not large, but it arrived in the right tempo, just enough boundary pressure to stop Ridgefield from crowding Felix, just enough composure to keep the required rate from becoming a talking point. When Henry Brooks eventually removed Brian, Eastport still needed only a small closing hand, and Kevin Young supplied it immediately, hitting cleanly enough in his short unbeaten ten to make sure the chase did not drift. Felix stayed there to the end, unbeaten on fifty-eight from forty-four, and the texture of that innings is what makes the player-of-the-match call straightforward. He was not only the top scorer in the chase. He was the batter who absorbed the only early danger, organized both major partnerships, and finished the job without allowing Ridgefield one final squeeze. Ridgefield’s bowlers had useful moments, especially Peter Shaw with the two early wickets and Thomas Wilson’s tidy spell in the middle, but they never had the cluster they needed after Felix settled. Logan Hayes removed Mark Harris, Henry Brooks removed Brian Martin, yet each wicket came after Eastport had already moved the chase into a safer lane. The contrast between the two innings is therefore clean. Ridgefield lost early wickets and had to rebuild through one main hand; Eastport lost early wickets too, but their main hand stayed longer, controlled more of the chase, and had enough support around him to prevent the innings from becoming fragmented. Ridgefield’s recovery was brave but limited. Eastport’s response was smaller in drama and larger in control. The scoreboard follows that logic: Ridgefield 118 all out after a top-order failure and a Jacob Ellis rescue; Eastport 121 for four after Felix Stone turned a brief wobble into a six-wicket match. The match was decided by which side handled instability better, and Eastport handled it better in both halves.
