Good morning again from Green Valley Park in the Midlands, where Ridgegate won the toss, chose to field, and arrived with every reason to believe they could pull Southmoor back into the same kind of nervous, low-scoring struggle that had defined the previous meeting, yet this time the match took a very different shape because Southmoor finally found what both sides had been missing for most of this little sequence: one proper top-order innings that understood the surface, understood the match, and stayed long enough to give every later over a purpose. Oliver Clark was the reason. He did not turn the first half of the innings into a rush, and that was the important part, because the pitch still had that awkward Green Valley feel where the ball held just enough, stopped just enough, and made cross-batted impatience look dangerous. Martin Reed was scratchy beside him, almost painfully slow at times, but his stay gave Southmoor a platform they had not enjoyed often enough in this rivalry. The first stand crawled, paused, restarted, and kept taking the free runs Ridgegate kept leaving around the pads and outside the hitting arc. It was not glamorous cricket. It was useful cricket. The opening pair moved Southmoor to fifty-eight before Martin was finally trapped in front by Christopher Bell, and the hidden value of that stand was that it forced Ridgegate to bowl deep into the innings without the reward of a cluster. Oliver had made only part of the stand himself, the extras had helped heavily, Martin had soaked up balls, and Ridgegate still had no way to say the toss had worked. Brian Vale came in and did not last long, run out by Marcus Hale after a short maintenance partnership, but by then Oliver had shifted from survival into control. Kevin Garcia then gave the innings its real kick. He did not need long. A boundary, one clean strike over the rope, and suddenly the field looked less tight than it had half an hour earlier. That partnership with Oliver was the hinge of the innings, because it moved Southmoor from a careful score into a defensible one and made Ridgegate’s earlier leakage look expensive rather than merely untidy. Garcia’s run-out at one hundred and thirteen gave Ridgegate a brief opening, but Oliver had already reached the phase where the bowling no longer looked as if it could own him. Elliot Martinez stayed with him for the last push, and Oliver’s final burst, before John Vale finally bowled him near the end, carried the innings to a very different emotional place. Southmoor closed on one hundred and thirty-seven for five, and in a where the surface never looked fully kind, that felt more than competitive. The total had one real spine, Oliver’s seventy-two from fifty-nine, six boundaries and three blows over the top, but it also had the right small supports: Martin’s time, Garcia’s sharp burst, Elliot’s little finish, and a large but decisive extras column that kept Ridgegate from tightening the match in the middle. Ridgegate’s bowlers were not poor across the board. John Vale was tidy and removed the main man. Christopher Bell gave them the first wicket. Robert Mills held a reasonable line. The problem was that the attack never found the double strike that makes a slow opening stand look foolish, and once Richard Lane and William Cross leaked at the wrong end of the innings, Southmoor had the cushion they needed. Chasing one hundred and thirty-eight, Ridgegate needed a clean start or at least one calm, extended partnership. They got neither for long enough. Robert Mills struck a boundary and looked briefly positive, but Andrew Lee had him caught behind with only twelve on the board, and that early wicket immediately made the chase feel less like a measured pursuit and more like another one of those low-score traps. William Cross and John Vale then produced Ridgegate’s best passage, a stand of twenty-eight that did not dominate but did at least give the innings a recognisable shape. John Vale made sixteen, William stayed long enough to suggest he might anchor the reply, and for a while the target looked manageable if not comfortable. Ronald Bell broke that calm by removing John, and after that Southmoor began to squeeze with a clarity Ridgegate had failed to find in the first innings. Marcus Hale came and went cheaply. Richard Lane briefly hit with intent, but George King removed him before that little spark could become momentum. Daniel Stone fell almost immediately after. William Cross, who had been holding the innings together more by presence than command, was bowled with Ridgegate only in the mid-sixties, and in that moment the chase effectively cracked. Joseph Turner and Thomas Reed disappeared in the same lower-order pressure wave, and at sixty-six for eight the target was no longer the main opponent. The opponent was panic. George King’s spell was the heart of that collapse, five wickets for very little, not just tail-end leftovers but the dismissals that kept Ridgegate from stitching together any late repair. Ronald Bell’s two wickets mattered in the middle. Paul Carter’s quick strike on Marcus Hale helped remove the captain before he could reset the chase. Jonah Walker later got Christopher Bell to shut down one more small partnership. Joshua Walker and Steven Wright did add some late dignity, and their final stand at least made Southmoor bowl out the full distance instead of walking through the last few overs untouched, but it never felt like a chase coming back to life. It felt like a scorecard being rescued from complete collapse. Joshua finished unbeaten, Steven flicked one late blow, and Ridgegate reached ninety-two for nine, still forty-five short and never close enough in the final quarter to put real stress back on Southmoor. That is the clearest difference between the two innings. Southmoor had one batter who stayed long enough to organize the entire card. Ridgegate had fragments. Southmoor’s best stand became a platform and then their third stand became acceleration. Ridgegate’s best stand became only a temporary pause before the middle caved in. Southmoor’s extras helped them build a total; Ridgegate’s extras only softened the defeat. So the result, Southmoor by forty-five runs and winners, felt properly earned. Oliver Clark was the obvious player of the match because his innings changed the whole tone of the contest. On a day when almost everyone else batted in pieces, he produced the one complete hand: patient enough to survive the slow start, strong enough to lift the death overs, and clear enough in judgment to turn a difficult surface into a winning score.
