At Kingsfield Park in Riverton, Southmere won the toss, chose to bat, and then spent most of the morning inside the kind of innings that never quite collapses in one spectacular burst yet keeps losing the ability to breathe, over by over, because Northford did not need constant drama to take control, only pressure repeated often enough that every batter began again from the same cramped place. Connor Blake gave Southmere their first small sign of movement, one early boundary and a few watchful leaves, but Jasper Vale was already operating on a line that made any forward movement feel conditional. The ball was not racing through, it was not doing something absurd every delivery, and that almost made the pressure worse, because the batters could not blame chaos. They simply had to keep defending good balls, keep waiting for a release, and keep discovering that the release was not arriving. Connor was trapped in front with the score barely into double figures, and Adrian Cole, who had been fighting rather than flowing, followed when Jasper came through him as well. Dominic Shaw tried to start again beside Ethan Price, but Harrison Cole’s entry kept the same claustrophobic rhythm intact, and when Dominic was taken by Caleb Frost, Southmere had already lost the right to treat this as a normal first innings. Benjamin Hart lasted only long enough to make one before Harrison pinned him too, and at four down for very little the match was no longer about setting a platform. It was about whether anyone could make the innings last long enough to become awkward. Ethan Price became that player. His twenty-nine from sixty-eight was not clean, not fast, not the sort of innings that looks impressive if read only as a number, but in this match it was the one hand that gave Southmere shape. He absorbed the hardest middle stretch, took the occasional boundary when Northford missed, and, most importantly, stayed beside Isaac Rowe long enough to produce the only partnership that looked like more than a pause in the collapse. Isaac’s own twelve from fifty was even more defensive in texture, almost stubborn to the point of being uncomfortable, but it mattered because it allowed Ethan to keep negotiating with the innings instead of being stranded in it. Together they added forty-three, with extras helping, and for a while Southmere could at least imagine dragging the total toward something that a nervous chase might dislike. Northford never let that thought grow beyond suggestion. Gavin Cross and Felix Dean kept the scoring blocked from the support end, Isaac Lowe absorbed overs without ever letting the game open up, and Harrison returned to remove Ethan at the exact moment the stand was beginning to look like a foundation rather than survival. That wicket broke the one stretch Southmere had fought hardest to build. Finley Grant disappeared almost immediately, Gavin Wells was caught off Jasper, Julian Tate was bowled by him too, and suddenly the long Ethan-Isaac passage felt less like recovery and more like the only thing standing between Southmere and a much smaller total. Harvey Stone gave the tail a few more minutes of stubbornness, finishing unbeaten on nine, and Leon Mercer added a late boundary before Logan Shaw ended the innings, but Southmere were still all out for ninety-one in the thirty-fifth over. The total had just enough bulk to demand a chase rather than a formality, though it was built from fragments: Ethan’s resistance, Isaac’s occupation, Harvey’s small not-out, and fifteen extras that made the score look less starved than the batting itself. Northford’s bowling card explained the day clearly. Harrison Cole’s four wickets were crucial because he broke the innings around Ethan’s resistance, especially in that phase where Southmere briefly seemed capable of staying intact. Jasper Vale, though, owned the match. Five wickets for eight from just under ten overs, with seven maidens, was not merely tidy. It was suffocating. He removed the early spine, came back for the lower-order attempts at delay, and never allowed Southmere to connect one piece of survival to the next for long enough to create momentum. Chasing ninety-two, Northford should have walked out with the match almost in hand, but tiny targets can become oddly sharp if the first few wickets come quickly, and Finley Grant made sure Southmere had one real burst of belief. Aaron Doyle struck one boundary and was bowled almost immediately. Caleb Frost came in and was pinned in front before he could settle. Dylan Kerr then tried to absorb the spell but only lasted until Ethan Price held the catch, and when Northford were three down with the score still in the teens, the atmosphere briefly changed. Southmere had not made enough runs, but they had created the one scenario in which the chase might become emotional rather than mathematical. That was where Blake Sutton and Ethan Vale did the most important batting of the second innings. Blake was calm from the first few overs after the damage, keeping his shape, leaving the ball that could be left, and refusing to let Finley’s burst turn into panic. Ethan Vale joined him with a little more authority, not reckless, but more willing to remind the fielding side that the target was still small and that a couple of clean shots could change the chase’s mood very quickly. Their partnership was worth sixty-three, and in a chase of this size that was decisive. Blake’s unbeaten thirty-five carried the quieter weight of the reply, five boundaries placed rather than forced, the kind of innings that stops a low target from becoming a mess. Ethan’s twenty-eight gave it the sharper edge, especially once the field had to spread and Southmere could no longer crowd the bat after those early wickets. Wides also hurt Southmere badly. A defence of ninety-one gives no room for generosity, yet loose balls kept softening the equation, and each extra made Finley Grant’s opening spell feel more isolated. Julian Tate eventually removed Ethan Vale with Harvey Stone taking the catch, but by then the match had already moved past its danger point. Felix Dean came in, stayed calm, and helped Blake Sutton finish the work. Northford reached ninety-four for four in the seventeenth over, a six-wicket win that looked comfortable by the end but had carried one genuine wobble at the start. That early wobble is important because it stops the match from being read as a simple procession. Southmere did fight. Finley Grant’s three wickets gave them their only real doorway. Ethan Price had earlier made the one innings that stopped their batting card from disappearing completely. Isaac Rowe had survived long enough to delay Northford’s full control. But delay was the limit of Southmere’s influence. They never built enough runs, never found a second partnership of substance, and never supported Finley’s new-ball burst with the discipline required to defend such a small target. Northford, by contrast, controlled almost every decisive phase. Jasper Vale and Harrison Cole made the first innings a long squeeze. The supporting bowlers made sure Ethan Price’s resistance stayed slow rather than dangerous. Then Blake Sutton and Ethan Vale absorbed the chase’s one dangerous moment and reduced the rest to process. Jasper Vale was the right player of the match because his spell was the event that made everything else possible. Without that pressure, Southmere’s one long stand might have become a real score. With it, they were kept to ninety-one, and even a three-wicket stumble in the reply was not enough to stop Northford moving through with room left.
