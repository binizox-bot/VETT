At Harborline Training Oval in Northport, Harborline XI chose to bat first and produced the kind of innings that did not need one frantic opening storm to become overwhelming, because the total was built in three clean layers: an early shove, a middle-overs spine, and a late partnership that made the chase feel heavy before Dune Riders had even started it. Kai Morgan and Logan Vale gave the innings its first push, not with a reckless powerplay but with enough clean hitting to deny Dune Riders a quiet opening spell. Kai’s nineteen from twelve had that quick, bright feel of a batter seeing the ball early, two boundaries and a lifted stroke over the rope before a run-out from Tariq Lane cut him off at twenty-four. That wicket was useful for Dune, because it stopped the first stand before it became fully damaging, but it did not really slow the innings for long. Logan Vale stayed assertive, Noah Blake settled beside him, and the second stand pushed Harborline past the first fielding squeeze. Logan’s twenty-six from fifteen, with three fours and two sixes, gave the top order enough aggression that Noah could begin more calmly, reading the surface rather than forcing his way into the innings. Martin Cole eventually had Logan caught at fifty-seven, and if Dune had backed that up immediately the first innings might have moved into a much more awkward shape. Instead, Noah Blake and Mason Hart built the passage that gave Harborline their real platform. Their stand of sixty was the most important construction phase of the innings, because it carried the score from a still-open fifty-seven for two to one hundred and seventeen for three, and it did so without making the batting side feel as though every over needed a risk. Mason’s twenty-two from twenty-four was not loud, but it was valuable in the way stabilizing hands often are. He stayed with Noah, kept the innings from becoming too dependent on one player, and allowed the captain-keeper to move from a settling role into a controlling one. Noah was the constant thread. His unbeaten sixty-eight from forty-seven was the innings that held every phase together: a few quiet balls early, then stronger rotation, then six fours and three sixes spread across the innings without ever looking like blind hitting. Martin Cole did strike again to remove Mason and then Ryan Cole, and Nabil Stone soon got Theo Grant, so at one hundred and twenty-five for five in the sixteenth over, there was still a window for Dune Riders to imagine holding Harborline under something more manageable. Luke Turner closed that window. His unbeaten thirty-two from seventeen was the finishing hand the innings needed, one boundary, three sixes, and no attempt to overcomplicate the task. With Noah still set, Luke could attack the right balls rather than manufacture shots from nothing, and their unbroken stand of fifty-one changed the final reading of the innings. A total that might have settled around the mid-one-fifties instead became one hundred and seventy-six for five. That is a very different chase psychologically. Dune’s bowling had one very good individual performance in Martin Cole’s three wickets, and Daryl Fox kept his spell tidy enough to stop the innings from completely running away earlier, but the attack as a whole could not sustain pressure through the last five overs. Six wides and a leg bye added only a little to the total, so the damage was mostly batting damage, and the key fact was that Harborline always found the next pair before Dune could turn one breakthrough into a cluster. The chase then collapsed almost before it had developed a stable shape, and that was almost entirely because Ronan Vale produced the kind of opening spell that rewrites a match. He removed Ishan Grant inside the first over, caught behind by Noah Blake, and then bowled Nate Wells soon after. Those two wickets immediately changed the emotional terms of the chase. Dune Riders were no longer asking how to pace one hundred and seventy-seven. They were asking how to survive long enough for that target to remain a cricket match. Blake Morgan tried to push back and did it better than most, making seventeen with three boundaries, but Ronan got him too, Logan Vale taking the catch, and when Connor Shaw removed Jordan Vale at thirty for four, the reply had already been broken into emergency pieces. The one period of resistance came from Cameron Scott and Leo Bennett. Cameron’s eighteen from eighteen had a compact, practical quality, while Leo’s thirteen from sixteen included the only real attempt to change the middle-overs rhythm with a six. Together they added thirty, which was the only stand in the innings that made Harborline work through more than a few balls of pressure. For a brief stretch, Dune Riders looked less like a side being swept away and more like a side trying to drag the game into the second half. Dylan Reed ended that idea. He removed Cameron at sixty and then Leo at sixty-four, and once that partnership was gone, the lower order had almost nothing left with which to resist. Owen Hayes stumped Evan Brooks for a duck, Ronan Vale returned to take out Tyler Quinn and Marcus Dean, and Caleb Frost finished the innings when Grant Walker was caught behind at seventy. The fall of wickets tells the real story in a way that no summary can soften: four down at thirty, a small recovery to sixty, then six wickets for ten runs across the back end. Ronan Vale’s four overs, two maidens, eleven runs, five wickets were the decisive performance of the match and fully explain the player-of-the-match award. These were not cheap wickets after the result was settled. He opened the wound with the first two, took Blake Morgan before the chase could stabilize, and returned to shut down any lower-order delay. Dylan Reed’s two wickets were also important because they broke the only real stand Dune Riders put together, and the rest of the attack stayed disciplined enough that no batter could use Ronan’s pressure as a chance to score freely elsewhere. Caleb Frost’s economy, Connor Shaw’s early support wicket, and Owen Hayes’ stumping all fitted into the same pattern: every time Dune Riders tried to form a second line, Harborline cut it. So the match resolves cleanly. Harborline XI batted first with controlled intent, built through Noah Blake, and finished hard through Luke Turner. Dune Riders answered with one brief middle stand and almost nothing sustained around it. The difference was not merely that Harborline scored more. It was that Harborline had batting continuity while Dune had batting fragments, and once Ronan Vale turned the chase into a procession of new batters, the margin could only grow. A one-hundred-and-six-run win is severe, and here it was justified by the shape of the whole game: one side built in layers, the other was dismantled in stages.
