At Meadow Park Oval in Westhaven, Westshore chose to bat first and almost immediately found themselves playing the innings from behind, because Eastport did not arrive with a loose new-ball spell or a wait-and-see plan; they went straight at the top order and kept the ball in the kind of channels that make young batters feel every defensive stroke has to be perfect. Ben Lawson was the first to go, caught from a short stay that never settled, and that early wicket gave Eastport the sound they wanted in the field. Aaron Vale tried to stay with Callum Marsh and absorb the next few overs, but Owen Hart’s line kept pulling the batters forward and back, never quite letting them decide whether they were defending the stumps or playing away from the body. Aaron’s dismissal left Westshore two down with the innings barely formed, and when Daniel Moss was pinned soon after, the match had already begun to tilt. Callum Marsh became the one player capable of giving Westshore some structure. He was not fluent in the clean, boundary-heavy sense, yet his innings had value because it stopped the card from tearing apart. Elliot Shaw gave him time for a while, mostly through occupation rather than scoring, and that stand bought a few overs without changing Eastport’s control. Ryan Blake removed Elliot just when the partnership was beginning to look stubborn, and Westshore again needed someone to do more than survive. Finn Mercer supplied that change. His partnership with Callum was the only phase of Westshore’s innings that felt like a genuine rebuilding passage rather than damage limitation. Callum kept holding one end, taking singles when the field opened and refusing to chase the ball that did not need to be chased, while Finn brought the first real forward movement, one clean boundary, one lifted stroke, and enough intent to make Eastport think about their field rather than simply attacking the next wicket. That stand carried Westshore beyond the worst of the early trouble, but it still never became a platform large enough to control the innings. Noah Reed came into the game at exactly the right moment for Eastport. He removed Callum after a long, resisting hand, then picked away at the lower middle order before Westshore could build another proper stand. George Nolan and Henry Cross tried to hang around with Finn, and the score moved in fragments, but the chase of respectability was always happening under pressure. Finn’s thirty-two gave Westshore their second meaningful hand, yet Connor Wells cut him off before the innings could turn toward a stronger final ten overs. Isaac Dale and Jack Holden then added late, necessary runs, the sort of lower-order partnership that does not change the balance of the match but prevents the first innings from looking completely undercooked. Owen Hart returned to remove Isaac, Ryan Blake finished Jack, and Leo Barrett was left stranded as Westshore were all out for one hundred and twenty-eight in the thirty-fifth over. The total had enough stubbornness inside it to require a professional chase, but it was still a score shaped by Eastport’s discipline more than Westshore’s command. Dylan Grant’s opening spell set the tone by taking the first wicket and giving very little away. Owen Hart did the heavier top-order damage. Ryan Blake struck at useful points. Connor Wells removed the one batter who might have dragged the total into a more awkward range. Noah Reed’s spell was the clearest reason the innings never fully recovered, because his three wickets cut into the exact section Westshore needed to hold together after Callum and Finn had done the repair work. Chasing one hundred and twenty-nine, Eastport did not try to make the pursuit look spectacular, and that was the correct decision. Ethan Cole and Liam Cross gave the reply a calm opening shape, with Liam playing the cleaner early hand and Ethan performing the quieter stabilizing role. Liam’s forty was the innings that removed most of the danger from the chase. He found the rope often enough to stop Westshore from crowding the bat, drove firmly when the ball was full, and kept the tempo above the point where the target could become irritating. Ethan was slower, but his occupation mattered because he allowed Liam to play with freedom without exposing the middle order too soon. The opening stand carried Eastport past the first major checkpoint, and even when Liam was run out by Callum Marsh, the chase had already been softened. Noah Reed then joined Ethan and played with exactly the temperament his earlier bowling spell suggested. He did not force the chase into drama. He worked the ball into gaps, let Ethan continue anchoring until Isaac Dale bowled him, and then adjusted again when Caleb Price came and went. Westshore had a few tidy spells, especially through Elliot Shaw and Henry Cross, and Isaac Dale’s wicket of Ethan gave them one moment where the innings might have become slightly nervous. Jack Holden later removed Caleb as well, which made Eastport bat a little deeper than they would have wanted. But the chase never genuinely escaped Eastport because Noah remained there. He had already shaped the first innings with the ball; now he shaped the finish with the bat. Mason Reed arrived and played the practical closing role, strong enough to punish the loose delivery and calm enough not to chase the target recklessly. Their unbroken stand took Eastport over the line in the twenty-sixth over, with seven wickets still in hand. The result was clear because Eastport won the key passages rather than relying on one isolated burst. They struck early with the ball, interrupted Westshore’s only true repair, kept the lower order from stretching the total too far, then chased with a stable opening stand and a composed finish. Westshore had honest resistance from Callum Marsh and Finn Mercer, plus a late scrap from Isaac Dale and Jack Holden, but they never owned enough overs in a row to make the match feel balanced. Noah Reed’s Player of the Match award fits cleanly because his three wickets were central to holding Westshore to a modest total, and his unbeaten hand in the chase ensured that the reply did not fray after the second and third wickets. Eastport were sharper with the ball, calmer with the bat, and more complete across the two innings.
