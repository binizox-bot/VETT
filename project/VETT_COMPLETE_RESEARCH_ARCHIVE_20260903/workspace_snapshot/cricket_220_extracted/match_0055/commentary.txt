At Greenfield Academy Ground in Midlands, Southmere won the toss, chose to bat, and then spent most of the afternoon discovering that possession of first use was worth very little once Eastford found their working length, because this was not a collapse made from one frantic over or one wild passage of panic, it was a slow tightening, a careful taking-away of options, the kind of innings where every batter appeared to arrive with the previous problem still sitting at the crease. Rowan Miles was the first to go, pinned by Harvey Wells after a short, uncomfortable stay, and Harvey’s opening spell set the emotional temperature of the innings more than the scorebook alone can show. He was not spraying around looking for magic. He was keeping the ball in areas where the batter had to make decisions, and on a surface that did not exactly fly onto the bat, that was enough to make even simple survival look like work. Elliot Mason stayed for a long time, though “stayed” is doing nearly all the work in that sentence, because his four from thirty-three was less an innings than a long refusal to leave, a vigil that slowed the card without really giving Southmere any control. Caleb Turner briefly looked like the one top-order batter prepared to play a shot with conviction, striking a boundary and trying to disturb the field, but Ethan Vale removed him before that little burst could become a phase, and when Finley Brooks later ended Mason’s occupation, Southmere had already spent more than ten overs without finding anything like rhythm. Felix Clarke came and went under Gavin Stone’s pressure, and that was the beginning of the spell that decided the first innings. Gavin did not bowl as if he needed to blast everyone out. He bowled as if he understood that Southmere had almost no scoring release left, and he simply kept returning the ball to those areas where patience starts to turn against the batter. Owen Brooks was bowled before he could settle. Riley Vance gave Parker Lane a few overs of companionship but was still forced into a mistake. Sawyer Grant then produced the one stretch that gave Southmere a faint claim to resistance, putting together the best partnership of the innings with Parker and finding two boundaries that at least made Eastford adjust the field for a while. Parker Lane’s own innings was the thread holding the whole thing together, and even though twenty-one not out sounds small in isolation, in context it was the only truly durable batting effort Southmere produced. He was not fluent. He was not dominant. He was simply there: defending, waiting, using the occasional gap, trying to turn a broken score into something irritating enough to bowl at. The trouble was that nobody stayed long enough with him. Sawyer fell to Gavin, Theo Reed flashed briefly before Julian Cross removed him, and Ethan Vale came back to close the last two wickets, Victor Shaw bowled and Wyatt Brooks trapped in front without scoring. Southmere were all out for eighty-one in the thirty-first over, and even that total owed plenty to Eastford’s own indiscipline, because seventeen extras, most of them wides, kept the innings from looking as bare as the batting really was. That detail matters. Eastford were not perfectly clean. They gave Southmere little scraps of air. But the important thing was that they kept taking wickets before those scraps could become structure. Harvey Wells created the initial squeeze with three maidens and almost nothing conceded. Ethan Vale collected three wickets and cleaned up important pieces at both ends of the innings. Finley Brooks broke the long but stalled occupation from Elliot Mason. Julian Cross chipped out Theo Reed when the lower order was trying to add nuisance value. And Gavin Stone, with four wickets in his full spell, owned the middle, the exact part of the innings where Parker Lane was trying to build something around himself. That is why the award made sense. Gavin did not just add numbers after the work had been done; he prevented Southmere’s only possible recovery from becoming more than a stubborn delay. Chasing eighty-two, Eastford should have had a simple job, but small targets in youth cricket always carry a strange little danger, because one early wicket makes everyone look at the scoreboard twice, and two early wickets can make a chase feel far more complicated than it has any right to be. Southmere did manage to create that brief discomfort. Theo Reed bowled beautifully with the new ball, finding the sort of discipline Southmere had lacked too often with the bat, and Adrian Cole never looked free before Parker Lane completed the catch to give Theo the breakthrough. Brandon Miles had not been exactly fluent either, and when Rowan Miles trapped him lbw soon after, Eastford were two down with the target still not completely erased. That was Southmere’s one doorway back into the match. They had a tiny score to defend, they had two wickets, and they had at least one bowler who looked capable of making the ball talk. What they did not have was enough collective discipline. Wides kept arriving, no-ball pressure crept in once, and every time Eastford might have been asked to earn a run properly, Southmere found a way to soften the equation. Connor Reed understood that better than anyone. He did not try to finish the chase in a rush. His unbeaten eighteen was a small innings, but it was exactly the kind of small innings that wins low chases: compact, patient, almost stubbornly free of unnecessary risk. Dominic Shaw then came in and changed the tone completely. He was the only batter in the chase who looked capable of making the target shrink in visible chunks, driving cleanly, using the short ball well enough, and striking the one six that finally made Southmere’s fielders feel as if the match was escaping rather than merely slipping. The third-wicket stand between Dominic and Connor did the real finishing work, not because it was huge in normal terms, but because in a chase of this size it gave Eastford certainty. Connor closed one end. Dominic advanced the other. The extras kept coming in the background like an unwanted echo of Southmere’s own batting innings. By the time Dominic was finally caught off Wyatt Brooks, the game had already been reduced to formalities. Ethan Vale arrived and ended it quickly, striking his first scoring shot with the clarity of a player who knew there was no value in dragging the afternoon out any longer, while Connor remained there to make sure nothing strange happened at the other end. Eastford reached eighty-two for three in the fifteenth over and won by seven wickets, a margin that felt fully accurate to the cricket despite the slightly uneasy opening to the chase. Southmere had fought in fragments: Parker Lane’s not-out resistance, Sawyer Grant’s brief support, Theo Reed’s excellent opening spell, Rowan Miles finding one lbw shout that stuck. But fragments were all they had. Their batting never found a partnership large enough to matter beyond delay, their extras gave Eastford too much of the chase for free, and their bowlers could not support Theo Reed long enough to make a tiny target feel genuinely alive. Eastford, by contrast, handled the day in phases. Harvey Wells pressed the top. Gavin Stone tore the middle out of the innings. Ethan Vale finished the tail. Connor Reed and Dominic Shaw then absorbed the only small scare in the chase and turned the result into process. So the match was not won by glamour, and it did not need to be. It was won by pressure, by timing, by enough control to make Southmere restart almost every few overs, and by the calm to avoid making eighty-two look like one hundred and twenty. Gavin Stone was the correct player of the match because his spell sat exactly where the match could still have changed shape. Parker Lane was trying to make Southmere awkward. Sawyer Grant briefly gave him help. Gavin removed that help, kept removing the next attempt after it, and made sure Eastford walked into the chase with a target that even a slightly nervous start could not spoil.
