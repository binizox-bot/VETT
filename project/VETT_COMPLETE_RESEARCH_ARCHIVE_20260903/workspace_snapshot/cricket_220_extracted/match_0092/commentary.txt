Good morning from Green Valley Park in the Midlands, where Ridgegate won the toss, chose to field, and ended up lifting the match by a single wicket after one of those low, nervous, ragged T20s where the scoreboard never looked large but somehow every run became heavier than it should have been, because Southmoor were nearly wrecked inside the first three overs, then somehow dragged themselves into a total with teeth, and Ridgegate, having done all the early damage with the ball, almost lost the match through the exact same problem they had created for the opposition: no one could make a chase feel calm for more than a few overs at a time. The first innings started with Ridgegate completely on top. John Vale was almost unplayable in terms of scoring pressure, bowling with that tight, stump-to-stump discipline that gives the batter no easy release, while Marcus Hale from the other end attacked with more bounce and more edge, slightly looser in wides but constantly threatening the wicket. Martin Reed went first, trapped in front before Southmoor had anything of substance on the board, Samuel Wright followed immediately, caught by Thomas Reed off Marcus Hale, and then Oliver Clark, who had already been stuck in place rather than settling, was pinned by John Vale to leave Southmoor three down with only three on the board. That opening collapse defined the whole morning because from then on Southmoor were never batting for a normal total. They were batting to make Ridgegate chase something awkward. Peter Lewis and Adam Lee offered the first modest repair, not an attacking stand, not even a comfortable one, but enough to slow the bleeding and get the innings out of immediate absurdity. Peter’s nine from fourteen was small but useful in that situation, Adam’s longer stay mattered more because he gave Southmoor somebody who could at least occupy the crease through the middle overs, and their sixteen-run partnership forced Ridgegate to keep working rather than simply wait for the innings to finish itself. Robert Mills broke that stand by removing Peter, and then added Jonah Walker not long after, which dragged Southmoor back into trouble almost as soon as they had begun to find breath. Kieran Hall briefly struck at the ball with more freedom than anyone before him, but Marcus Hale returned to bowl him and make the score thirty-four for six, and when Kevin Allen was run out soon after, Southmoor were thirty-six for seven with almost no batting shape left. At that point the innings should probably have disappeared. It did not, and that is why Brendan Young’s role deserves to be read properly. He was not producing a glamorous rescue, but he was the one batter who understood the value of simply refusing to leave. Adam Lee stayed a little longer beside him and helped add another small patch before Richard Lane finally got him, but the major salvage came once George King joined Brendan. Their ninth-wicket stand of thirty-three changed the match. It was not pure batting authority, because five extras sat inside it, but it was the only partnership in the Southmoor innings that lasted long enough to make Ridgegate think about the scoreboard rather than only the wickets. George made nine from twenty, Brendan carried the cleaner weight with a controlled unbeaten twenty-three, and suddenly what had looked like a score in the fifties had become something in the high seventies. William Cross eventually removed George at the end of the nineteenth over, and Thomas Reed finished the innings by having Elliot Scott caught by Marcus Hale, but the total had reached eighty-one, and in a match where the pitch, the pressure and the timing all looked awkward, that was not nothing. Ridgegate’s bowling card still looked dominant: John Vale gave away almost nothing, Marcus Hale took three wickets in the passages that mattered most, Robert Mills broke the lower top order, and every supporting bowler found a way into the game. Yet the thirteen wides and the late ninth-wicket resistance meant Southmoor had been allowed back from near-extinction. The chase then proved exactly how costly that could be. Ridgegate needed only eighty-two, but from the first over the pursuit felt nervous. Robert Mills went cheaply, caught behind off Andrew Vale, and although Richard Lane and William Cross tried to settle the reply, George King removed Richard before the fourth over was done. Marcus Hale and William added fifteen, with extras doing a large part of the movement, but Brian Young, already important with the bat, removed Marcus and opened the door again. Kieran Hall then produced the spell that almost stole the match outright. Charles Grant fell to him, William Cross was later taken by Brian Young, and Ridgegate were forty-three for five, still more than halfway from the target and suddenly looking nothing like the side that had reduced Southmoor to ruins earlier. John Vale and Daniel Stone produced the most important calm passage of the chase. Their sixteen-run stand had no extras in it, which made it one of the few genuinely controlled batting phases of the match, John playing the more active hand with fifteen and Daniel beginning the long, stubborn stay that eventually carried Ridgegate over the line. But Paul Carter bowled John, Kieran Hall removed Matthew Lewis, and then Kieran struck again to get Christopher Bell, leaving Ridgegate seventy-two for eight and still not safe. Daniel Stone kept refusing the panic around him. His unbeaten fourteen from twenty-three was not large, but in this chase it was enormous because every time Ridgegate lost another partner, he kept the next ball small, the next run possible, the next mistake avoidable. Thomas Reed helped edge the score closer with a short stand, only for George King to have him caught with Ridgegate still one blow away from embarrassment, seventy-nine for nine, two needed, one wicket left, and the match suddenly belonging to the last pair. The ending was appropriately ugly. There was no heroic boundary, no clean finish, no sweeping image of command. Daniel Stone and Joseph Turner survived the last passage, the final movement came through pressure, extras and a little scrambled control, and Ridgegate crept to eighty-two for nine with just enough balls unused to make the scorecard show victory rather than panic. Southmoor’s defence was exceptional for a team defending such a small total: Kieran Hall’s three wickets for eight were nearly match-winning, George King took two and kept the final over alive, Brian Young backed up his batting rescue with two wickets, and Andrew Vale struck first. But Ridgegate had done enough earlier, and Marcus Hale’s opening burst remained the reason Southmoor never had a proper total to defend. That is why the player-of-the-match award stayed with him. His three wickets smashed the top order before Southmoor had even begun, and although the chase nearly turned into a disaster, those early blows created the tiny target that Ridgegate eventually stumbled past. The whole match was a mess of pressure, wides, small partnerships, and broken nerves, but the structure is clear: Southmoor recovered from three for three and thirty-six for seven to post eighty-one, Ridgegate nearly wasted the chase by falling to nine down, and Daniel Stone’s late composure plus Marcus Hale’s first-innings damage left Ridgegate winners by one wicket in a final that felt far bigger than its score.
