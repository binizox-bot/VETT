At Oakmere College Oval in Westport, Harborstone won the toss and chose to field, and the match then developed into the kind of slow-pressure game where the first innings did not collapse in a spectacular rush, yet still never achieved anything close to batting control. Ridgewell used the full fifty overs, which on the surface can make the innings look durable, but the deeper reading is much harsher because the batters rarely owned the scoreboard themselves. Kai Mercer and Tomas Reed began with a little movement, helped by early extras, before Kai was run out by Noah Kent with the score on sixteen, and that first wicket already hinted at the pattern of the innings: Ridgewell could survive for periods, but they were not forcing Harborstone to change plan. Tomas then fell to Caleb Price after making only nine, Arun Hale came in to occupy rather than dominate, and Nolan Frost, the captain, produced the first meaningful batting passage of the innings. His twenty-four from thirty-one was the best Ridgewell score and the one phase where the batting side looked as though it might escape the purely defensive rhythm, especially during the thirty-five-run stand with Arun. Even there, though, the stand was partly inflated by extras, and Harborstone never looked stretched. Logan Hayes broke that partnership by removing Nolan, Noah Kent then took out Arun, and when Milo Grant followed without scoring, Ridgewell had slipped from sixty-one for three to sixty-six for five, a small but important collapse that cut away their only genuine middle-over base. Evan Rowe and Rafi Stone tried to rebuild through time rather than tempo, and their stand added twenty-eight, but it was hard to call it a counterattack. Evan made eight from thirty-five, Rafi did most of the scoring with nineteen inside that pair, and Harborstone were content to keep the field up enough to make every single feel earned. Caleb Price bowled Evan at ninety-four, Logan Hayes removed Rafi at one hundred and twelve, and from there Ridgewell’s last stretch became an exercise in dragging the innings to the full quota rather than setting a demanding target. Dylan Shore faced sixty-four balls for thirteen, Ben Ellis faced forty-five for nine, Carter Wells and Hugo Lane were there at the end, and the score reached one hundred and thirty-one for nine only because the innings kept absorbing deliveries and the extras column kept swelling. That is the most important hidden detail in the first innings: thirty-two extras in a total of one hundred and thirty-one, with twenty-eight wides, meant Ridgewell made only ninety-nine from the bat across fifty overs. Harborstone’s bowlers therefore deserve credit not only for taking wickets but for keeping every partnership provisional. Logan Hayes was the obvious wicket-taker with three, Caleb Price added two, Dylan Fox and Mason Brooks each struck once, and Noah Kent’s spell was the quiet center of control: ten overs, four maidens, only twelve runs, one wicket, and the run-out involvement that started the card’s first break. Aaron Lane’s side did not bowl a perfect innings because the wides were far too many, but they bowled enough controlled balls between those errors that Ridgewell never found a period of command. Chasing one hundred and thirty-two, Harborstone did not need to hurry, and Noah Kent made sure they did not mistake a modest target for permission to be loose. Ethan Brook opened with him and played one of those slow support hands that can look ugly in isolation but make sense inside a low chase. Ethan’s twelve from sixty-four did not add tempo, but it gave Noah a stable partner and, with Ridgewell giving away extras of their own, the chase kept moving without risk. Noah was the actual author of the innings. He took width when it came, drove cleanly through the off side, used the full ball better than anyone in the match, and never let Ridgewell believe that one tight over could turn into a real squeeze. The opening stand reached ninety-one before Carter Wells finally had Ethan caught by Nolan Frost, but by then the target had already been reduced to a formality. That stand also carried twenty-seven extras, which says plenty about Ridgewell’s inability to defend a low total: they needed pressure from every ball, yet kept giving Harborstone free movement. Samir Vale came in after the lone wicket and played the finishing role cleanly. Noah, already set, moved through the last phase with complete calm, adding twenty-six of the final partnership himself, while Samir’s unbeaten thirteen ensured there was no late wobble. Harborstone reached one hundred and thirty-two for one in the thirty-first over, and the nine-wicket margin fits the actual feel of the chase. Ridgewell had batted the full fifty overs, but they had not built a score with weight. Harborstone had conceded too many extras, but their bowling phases were still far more disciplined than Ridgewell’s batting phases. Then, with the bat, Noah Kent produced the only genuinely commanding innings of the day, finishing seventy-eight not out after earlier delivering the most economical full spell of the match. That is why the Player of the Match call is straightforward. Logan Hayes’ three wickets broke important sections of the first innings, Caleb Price supported with two, and Ethan Brook’s occupation had contextual value, but Noah Kent was the player who connected both halves of the match: he strangled Ridgewell’s scoring when they were trying to survive, helped create the first dismissal, and then carried the chase so thoroughly that Harborstone won by nine wickets without ever giving Ridgewell a serious route back.
