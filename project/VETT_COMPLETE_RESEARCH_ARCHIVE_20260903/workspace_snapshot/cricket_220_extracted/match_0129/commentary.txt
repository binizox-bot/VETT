At Red Mesa Arena in Eastmere, Southport won the toss and chose to field in this fifteen-over contest, and for a couple of deliveries that decision looked as if it had immediate reward written all over it, because Arden Cole was pinned in front by Xavier Holt before Westvale had even settled into the idea of building an innings. One for one in the first over is exactly the kind of start a fielding side wants in a shortened match. It creates urgency, it invites one more attacking field, and it makes the batting side decide very quickly whether to rebuild or punch back. Blake Vale chose the second option. His stay was brief, almost too brief to become a full innings, but twenty-two from seven balls was still enough to change the tone completely. He hit two fours and two sixes, and for that small passage Southport’s early wicket lost most of its comfort because Westvale were no longer creeping away from trouble; they were counterattacking it. Warren Chase removed Blake with Victor Shaw holding the catch, and that left the card at thirty-one for two, which still looked like a position Southport could control if they could break the next stand quickly. They could not. Caden Mills became the centre of Westvale’s innings, not by producing a wild, uninterrupted assault, but by holding together an innings that kept losing pieces around him. He first worked with Dylan Ross, then watched Dylan go caught and bowled to Sawyer Glenn. Evan Brooks then gave Westvale a short burst, thirteen from seven, but Oliver Kent removed him before he could grow into anything larger. Felix Ward made a useful nine, Gavin Pierce flashed eleven from five, and still wickets kept falling every time Westvale looked ready to build a clean second platform. That is why Caden’s fifty from thirty-two balls mattered so much. He was the one batter who kept the innings coherent between Blake’s early violence and Hudson Clark’s late push. Caden did not let Southport turn wickets into a squeeze. Even when partners came and went, he kept finding enough boundaries and enough rotation to prevent the score from dropping into survival mode. At one hundred and eight for six, though, Westvale were still vulnerable. In a fifteen-over match that is a dangerous place to be: plenty of runs on the board, yes, but also very little margin for a quiet finish. Hudson Clark changed that finish. His unbeaten thirty-six from twenty balls was the innings’ second decisive hand, because it was not just late decoration after the platform was already safe. It was the difference between a competitive score and a genuinely heavy one. He first steadied the innings beside Caden, then widened his scoring arc once Southport’s wicket-taking threat began to soften. Caden finally fell at one hundred and fifty-one, caught and bowled by Xavier Holt, but by then Hudson had already done enough to carry the total into strong territory. Ian Forde’s unbeaten five and the final twelve-run stand gave the innings one last nudge, and Westvale closed on one hundred and sixty-three for seven. Southport had taken wickets regularly enough to feel involved throughout. Xavier Holt struck with the first wicket and later removed Caden. Quentin Hale took two in the middle. Warren Chase, Oliver Kent, and Sawyer Glenn all found one each. The problem was that the wickets never became control. Westvale kept answering each break with a burst, a stabilizing hand, or a late push, and that made the target look larger than the seven wickets suggested. Chasing one hundred and sixty-four, Southport needed a clean start. Felix Ward gave them the opposite. Oliver Kent was stumped by Dylan Ross almost immediately, Noah Price was then caught behind after giving the chase its first real movement, and Parker Stone followed caught by Ian Forde. All three belonged to Felix Ward, and that spell of three for eleven was the decisive phase of the match. It did not end the chase mathematically, but it changed the type of chase Southport had to play. They were no longer pacing a target. They were repairing one. Noah’s thirty-four from nineteen gave them life, especially in the partnership with Parker Stone, and their fifty-run stand from the early damage was the one phase where Southport looked as if they might drag the match back toward balance. But because Felix had already taken the first wicket, then the second, then the third, every recovery carried a little more pressure than it should have. Quentin Hale and Riley Brooks then gave Southport their best middle-overs answer. Quentin’s twenty-seven had control and intent, while Riley’s twenty-three from sixteen kept the rate from becoming impossible. Their stand of thirty-seven took the score to one hundred and ten, and that was the last stage where the chase still had genuine shape. Evan Brooks broke it by removing Riley, and Gavin Pierce then removed Quentin at one hundred and twenty-six. Those two wickets were not as spectacular as Felix’s new-ball burst, but they were nearly as important in match terms because they stopped Southport from converting a recovery into a finish. Sawyer Glenn did what he could from the lower middle order, staying unbeaten on twenty, and Theo Mason briefly added one more spark before Logan West removed him. Victor Shaw’s final boundary made the score look a little closer, but Southport finished on one hundred and forty-one for six, twenty-two runs short. The difference between the sides was not that Westvale batted flawlessly or that Southport folded without resistance. Westvale were in trouble more than once. Southport fought through Noah, Quentin, Riley, and Sawyer. The difference was that Westvale found the more valuable interventions at the sharper moments: Blake Vale’s immediate counter after the first-ball wicket, Caden Mills’ stabilizing fifty through the middle chaos, Hudson Clark’s unbeaten finish, and then Felix Ward’s three-wicket burst that forced the chase onto the wrong track before it could settle. That is why Felix Ward as Player of the Match fits the reconstructed scorecard cleanly. His batting contribution was only a small piece of the first innings, but his bowling spell decided the emotional direction of the second. Southport kept chasing after that, but they were always chasing repairs as much as runs, and in a shortened match that burden is usually too heavy.
