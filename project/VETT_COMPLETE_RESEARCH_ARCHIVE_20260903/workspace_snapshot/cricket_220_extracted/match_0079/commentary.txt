At Central Academy Oval 2 in Westport, Highland Vale won the toss, chose to field, and for the first seven overs they had almost exactly the game they wanted, because Northbay were not easing into a clean batting day at all, they were being shoved into crisis, first after Ethan Cole’s sharp little burst ended before it could become a launch, then after Noah Grant was taken out just as he seemed to have survived the first movement, and then with Owen Blake bowled straight away, leaving the scoreboard at thirty-nine for three and the innings suddenly looking less like a normal first-innings build and more like a repair job that had to begin immediately. Ethan’s eighteen from a dozen balls had briefly given Northbay some edge, two crisp boundaries and one early lift over the field, but his wicket at twenty-two took away that tempo. Noah’s dismissal a few overs later did more structural damage because Lucas Hart had only just begun to settle, and when Owen Blake disappeared first ball the match entered its defining phase. Lucas Hart did not respond by rushing. That was the important thing. He played as if the whole afternoon had to be rebuilt one small piece at a time, leaving what he could, absorbing the fuller length, and making sure Highland Vale’s early burst did not turn into a complete first-hour collapse. Caleb Fox was the first partner who gave that rebuild real shape. His thirty-eight from sixty-six was not a loud innings, and it did not need to be. It gave Lucas the thing Northbay most lacked after three wickets had fallen: time. Their fourth-wicket stand of eighty-eight took the score from danger into stability, and although a handful of extras helped the progress, most of the work was done through patience, placement and a refusal to give Highland Vale the next obvious wicket. Caleb eventually fell at one hundred and twenty-seven, caught by Oliver Dean off Henry Ward, and that was a useful breakthrough for the fielding side, yet by then the innings had already changed. Northbay were no longer trying to avoid embarrassment. They had a set batter, overs left, and a platform that could be expanded. Ryan Turner then arrived and changed the tempo in the most important passage of the innings. Lucas Hart stayed in the anchor’s role, still measuring the innings, still stretching it forward, while Ryan went after the loose ball with far more urgency. Their fifth-wicket stand of ninety-two was the moment the match tilted for good. Ryan made fifty-six from forty-two, five fours and two sixes, the kind of innings that compresses pressure back onto a bowling side because every defensive over suddenly has to be followed by a boundary-saving one. Lucas added the quieter share inside that partnership, and that balance was exactly what made the stand so damaging. Highland Vale could not attack both ends in the same way. If they pushed too hard at Lucas, he absorbed. If they missed at Ryan, he punished. By the time Oliver Dean removed Ryan at two hundred and nineteen, Northbay had travelled from early trouble to a total that already looked hard to chase. The closing overs then became a matter of Lucas Hart supervising the innings while the lower order added useful scraps around him. Mason Reed stayed briefly, Logan Price struck a quick ten, Connor Shaw added a small but valuable seven, and Jordan Wells was there at the end while Lucas remained unbeaten on one hundred and four. That innings was the whole spine of Northbay’s day: not just a hundred, not just a long stay, but the continuous thread from the early collapse through the middle repair and into the final total of two hundred and sixty-eight for eight. Highland Vale’s bowling had merit. Samuel Price created early damage. Henry Ward was controlled through the middle. Oliver Dean’s two wickets around the Ryan Turner phase helped stop the innings becoming even larger. Tyler Ward was steady enough without appearing here as a headline figure, and Ben Cooper had the early wicket that interrupted Ethan Cole. Yet the larger fact was that Highland Vale had Northbay at thirty-nine for three and still let them reach a genuinely defendable score, mainly because they could not remove Lucas Hart and could not contain Ryan Turner when the innings moved into its acceleration phase. Chasing two hundred and sixty-nine, Highland Vale needed a stable start, and they did not get one. Aiden Scott fell almost immediately, caught behind off Jordan Wells, and that wicket meant Daniel Cross had to begin his innings with the chase already leaning on him. Evan Miles gave the reply a flash of momentum, making seventeen with three boundaries and helping add thirty-six for the second wicket, but Jordan Wells struck again to remove him, and the pattern of the chase began to show itself early: Daniel Cross could keep the innings alive, but Northbay kept removing the partner who looked most able to make his work matter. Jacob Hayes gave the chase its first proper controlled phase. His twenty-two from thirty-eight was useful because it took Highland Vale to seventy-eight and let Daniel settle into the longer role, but Logan Price removed him at exactly the point where that stand might have become a platform. Liam Carter and Nathan Reed then both fell to Connor Shaw in quick succession, and that was the first major fracture of the chase. From seventy-eight for three to ninety-three for five, Highland Vale lost the middle support that could have let Daniel Cross play with patience rather than pressure. Ben Cooper’s nineteen briefly changed the rhythm, two clean blows over the field giving the chase a little spark, and his stand with Daniel was one of the cleaner phases because it came entirely from the bat. Mason Reed broke it at one hundred and twenty-three, and Henry Ward then gave Daniel another short repair before Mason struck again. At one hundred and forty-eight for seven the chase looked almost done, but Daniel Cross still refused to let it disappear quietly. His partnership with Oliver Dean was the last real threat Northbay had to manage. Daniel scored the bulk of that stand, driving and cutting with increasing force, and for a few overs the chase had the kind of late shape that can make a defending side tighten up. Oliver’s eleven was modest, yet it mattered because it allowed Daniel to dominate the scoring. Connor Shaw ended that danger by having Oliver stumped by Ryan Turner at one hundred and ninety-one. Once that wicket fell, Highland Vale had no real route left. Samuel Price went cheaply, Eli Foster stayed to the end, and Daniel Cross was finally dismissed for ninety-four, caught by Ryan Turner off Jordan Wells, ending the innings at two hundred in the forty-third over. The scorecard shows a chase that had one outstanding hand and too many broken supports. Daniel Cross carried the innings almost from the start, but Evan Miles, Jacob Hayes, Ben Cooper, Henry Ward and Oliver Dean all fell before any of them could become the second pillar the chase required. Northbay’s bowling worked because it attacked those supports with excellent timing. Jordan Wells framed the innings with three wickets, taking the first, the second, and finally Daniel himself. Connor Shaw’s three wickets cut through the middle and late middle, especially the pair that turned a workable position into a deep problem. Mason Reed removed Ben Cooper and Henry Ward, the two lower-middle partners who might have helped Daniel push the chase deeper. Logan Price’s wicket of Jacob Hayes mattered because it broke the last top-order stabilizing stand. Tyler Brooks then made sure there was no tail-end nuisance. That is why the final margin of sixty-eight runs felt earned rather than inflated. Northbay were in trouble early, rebuilt through Lucas Hart and Caleb Fox, surged through Ryan Turner, and then defended by repeatedly asking Highland Vale the same question: who stays with Daniel Cross long enough to turn his innings into a chase? Highland Vale never found that answer for more than a few overs at a time, and Northbay turned a dangerous start at thirty-nine for three into a controlled sixty-eight-run win.
