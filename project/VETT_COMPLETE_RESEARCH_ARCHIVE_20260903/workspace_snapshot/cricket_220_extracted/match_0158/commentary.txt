At Harborline Training Oval in Northport, this semi-final carried the familiar weight of a knockout game where one strong innings can keep a side alive for a long time, yet one bowling spell can still change the entire meaning of that innings, and that is exactly how Crestfall A’s first effort unfolded after they chose to bat. They were not blown away early, and for a while Rohan Vale gave them enough presence at the top to suggest they might build something properly awkward, but Harborline A kept striking just often enough to prevent the batting card from ever becoming fully settled. Elias Brook was the first to go after a small opening stand, caught with the score at eighteen, and although Jordan Pike briefly helped Rohan move the innings into the forties, Malik Ford’s wicket of the captain-keeper and then the quick removal of Finn Carter meant Crestfall had reached the end of the powerplay with one batter carrying almost all the forward motion. Rohan understood that position well. His sixty-three from forty-six was not a reckless innings, and it was not a passive one either. He picked his moments, used the boundary when Harborline missed too straight or too full, and kept the innings attached to a realistic total even while partners around him kept arriving under pressure. The most important repair came with Nolan Price. Their stand of forty-six was the one passage where Crestfall A looked as though they might push beyond simple survival, because Nolan’s seventeen from sixteen gave Rohan some room to breathe and the score moved from forty-two for three to eighty-eight for four without another collapse. Yet that was also where Ronan Vale began to decide the match. He had Nolan stumped at exactly the moment the partnership was threatening to become a platform, then soon after trapped Theo Lane in front and removed Owen Miles, turning a useful middle-overs position into a much more fragile one. Crestfall were not dead at that point, because Rohan was still there and Caleb Ross added a small hand, but the innings no longer had the look of a side building toward a heavy semi-final total. It had become a side trying to drag itself into something defendable. Ronan’s fourth wicket, the dismissal of Rohan himself at one hundred and seventeen, was the decisive break. Once the set batter went, Crestfall lost the one player who had connected every phase of the innings. Caleb Ross and Dylan Stone added a few, Marcus Ford helped Dylan nudge the close, and the last stretch did carry the total to one hundred and forty-two for eight, but the innings felt short of what Rohan’s work had briefly made possible. Harborline’s bowling was strong because the pressure was layered. Jasper Cole took two wickets and kept the first innings from opening out late. Malik Ford’s two early-middle wickets cut into the top order. Theo Miles and Ben Archer gave very little away in their supporting spells. Owen Stark was more expensive, but even those runs did not become fatal because Ronan kept arriving with the wicket that mattered. Four for twenty-five in a semi-final is already a valuable line, but the timing made it far more than a line. Nolan, Theo, Owen and Rohan were the exact wickets Crestfall needed not to lose in that phase, and Ronan removed all four. Chasing one hundred and forty-three, Harborline did have one unusual early wobble when Kieran Moss was run out at twenty-eight, but Ellis Grant’s intent made sure that wicket never became a real squeeze. Ellis batted as if the target had to be cut down early before Crestfall could build belief, and his forty-six from twenty-two did exactly that. Two fours and four sixes gave the chase its first shape, but the value of his innings was not only in the boundaries. It was in the pressure he removed from Carter Wells. Carter could settle, watch the pace of the pitch, and then build the chase in the more measured rhythm that would eventually win it. Their stand of sixty-four was the phase that broke Crestfall’s defense. By the time Ellis was caught at ninety-two, the target had already been pulled into manageable territory, and although Mason Reid fell soon after for eight, that wicket did not restore enough tension because Carter was still there. He played the innings Harborline needed in the second half: not loud, not careless, and not tempted into finishing too early. His unbeaten fifty-three from forty-eight gave the chase continuity through every small interruption, and when Ryan Frost joined him, the final act became controlled rather than frantic. Ryan’s unbeaten eighteen from thirteen was the right finishing hand, firm enough to keep the board moving, calm enough not to create another opening. Crestfall’s bowlers had individual moments. Marcus Ford removed the dangerous Ellis, Caleb Ross dismissed Mason, and some overs from Caleb and Marcus briefly slowed the scoring. But they never created the cluster a defense of one hundred and forty-two required. Simon Reed, Dylan Stone and Theo Lane all went at rates that kept Harborline ahead of the chase, and Finn Carter’s lone over gave away enough extras to make the target shrink even faster. The difference between the two innings was therefore structural. Crestfall had Rohan Vale holding one side together while Harborline kept breaking the rest. Harborline had Ellis Grant breaking the chase open and Carter Wells holding the rest of it steady. Ronan Vale’s spell had already lowered the target into a range where one good chase partnership could decide the match, and Ellis with Carter provided exactly that. So Harborline A reached one hundred and forty-five for three in the eighteenth over, won by seven wickets, and moved through because they controlled the two central passages of the semi-final: Ronan Vale’s middle-late spell against Crestfall’s batting spine, and the Ellis-Carter chase stand that prevented the target from ever growing teeth.
