At Brookfield Oval in Fairhaven, Westmoor chose to field first and eventually won by four wickets, but the shape of the match was more layered than that margin suggests, because Ridgefield were not simply rolled over from the first over and Westmoor did not chase the target without turbulence; instead the game passed through three distinct moods, an early Ridgefield repair after a poor start, a long middle squeeze led by Lewis Archer, and then a chase that looked almost settled before Dylan Foster dragged a little late disorder back into it. The first innings began with exactly the sort of dismissal Westmoor wanted after choosing to bowl, Toby Lane hitting the right length early and removing Aiden Moss before Ridgefield had any real rhythm, but Blake Warren immediately changed the temperature of the innings. He was busy, almost too eager at times, but his twenty-four from twenty balls gave Ridgefield the only truly sharp top-order burst they managed all morning, four boundaries and a lifted shot that forced Westmoor to adjust the field sooner than they would have liked. Caleb Stone came in and looked far more durable, less frantic than Blake, much clearer about the long work required, and for a little while those two gave Ridgefield a way out of the opening dent. Toby returned to remove Blake, Kian Brooks taking the catch, and that wicket stopped the early scoring from becoming a full platform, but it did not yet give Westmoor control. That control had to be earned later, because Caleb Stone and Dylan Foster then produced Ridgefield’s one genuinely substantial passage of batting. Caleb was the player most likely to turn the innings into something competitive, not because he scored wildly, but because he kept finding the right moments to release pressure. His forty-nine from sixty-one had enough boundary threat to make the field move, and the two clean hits over the rope gave the stand an energy Dylan Foster could not have supplied on his own. Dylan’s role was different. He was slower, tighter, more about occupation than command, but that mattered in the context of Ridgefield’s innings. He stayed with Caleb through the only phase when the batting side looked as though it might build a total large enough to make the chase uncomfortable. Their stand reached sixty-one, and for a time Westmoor were having to bowl at two batters who had taken the innings beyond survival and into construction. The turning point came when Kian Brooks removed Caleb just short of his fifty, Mason Cole taking the catch, because that dismissal cut away the only Ridgefield batter who looked capable of pushing the score toward something meaningfully above the mid-hundreds. From there Lewis Archer began to control the match. He did not do it with noise. He did it with a spell that kept shrinking Ridgefield’s options. Dylan Foster, after all that patient work, was bowled before he could turn occupation into acceleration. Ethan Hart followed after a brief, careful stay, also beaten by Lewis, and that pair of wickets changed the innings from a possible platform into a repair job all over again. Finley Rhodes and Gavin Reed could not rebuild it. Harvey Stone removed Finley, then Lewis came back to take Gavin, and suddenly Ridgefield had slipped from a position that still carried promise into one where every lower-order run felt like salvage. Hayden Cole and Isaac Dale did give them one more stubborn partnership, not flashy, not fluent, but useful because it dragged the total away from complete underperformance. Hayden found a couple of boundaries in his thirteen, Isaac wore down a few overs for seven, and together they added enough to push Ridgefield near the kind of score that at least asks a chase to concentrate. Harvey broke that stand by trapping Hayden, Mason Cole then finished the last stretch by bowling Isaac and having Logan Vale caught, and Ridgefield were all out for one hundred and fifty-two in the forty-third over. It was a total made from one serious hand, one early spark, one adhesive support innings and a few late scraps. The extras helped, but not enough to disguise the larger truth: after Caleb Stone went, Ridgefield never again looked like they owned the innings. Westmoor’s bowling card carried that message clearly. Toby Lane was expensive but made the first two incisions. Kian Brooks’ wicket of Caleb was a major structural break. Harvey Stone was the quiet destroyer of lower-order hope. Mason Cole closed the innings neatly. Lewis Archer was the reason the whole middle phase bent toward Westmoor. His ten overs brought three wickets for twenty-two, two maidens, and a kind of control that meant Ridgefield had to earn even the small runs after the one real stand was broken. Chasing one hundred and fifty-three, Westmoor then started as though they had no interest in letting a middling target acquire drama. Oliver Hayes and Callum Price put on seventy-six for the first wicket, and that partnership was the match’s second decisive passage. Oliver played the measured hand, not rushing, taking time to settle, then working the chase down through singles and the occasional boundary. Callum was brighter and more direct, cutting and driving whenever Ridgefield missed, and because he scored at the better tempo, Westmoor never got stuck even when Oliver absorbed a few quiet balls. Ridgefield also made the chase easier than it needed to be. Wides appeared at exactly the wrong time, and in a defence of this size those loose runs become more than mistakes; they become pressure removed from the batters. Ten extras arrived in the opening stand alone, and by the time Oliver was caught by Caleb Stone off Aiden Moss, Westmoor had already taken the hardest part out of the target. Callum followed soon after to Dylan Foster, and that was the first hint that the chase might not be as smooth as the opening pair had made it appear. Reece Holden and Nathan Ford tried to settle it, Nathan playing cautiously and Reece working the ball around, but Aiden Moss removed Nathan with Blake Warren taking the catch, and Dylan Foster then pulled the innings into its only true wobble. He had Reece caught by Hayden Cole, then later removed Lewis Archer cheaply, and suddenly Westmoor’s middle order had to do more than simply admire the openers’ work. Kian Brooks helped ensure that the situation never became a crisis. His eighteen from fourteen was short, but it carried exactly the right energy, a six to ease the pressure, hard running, and enough intent to prevent Ridgefield from crowding the bat after each wicket. Dylan Foster still kept fighting. His fourth wicket, Kian caught by Logan Vale, left Westmoor six down and gave Ridgefield the scoreboard respect they had been chasing since the opening stand broke. But by then the target was too small and the earlier damage too deep. Toby Lane came in with the calm of a lower-order batter who understood the equation, not trying to finish with one swing, not letting the late wickets create a false panic. He stayed unbeaten on fifteen, found two boundaries, and with Elliot Rowe’s neat little unbeaten nine, took Westmoor over the line in the twenty-sixth over. Ridgefield could point to Dylan Foster’s four-wicket spell and say they had made a contest out of the chase, and that would be fair. He was comfortably their best bowler and, with his earlier twenty-three, their most complete losing-side contributor. Aiden Moss also found two wickets, though his runs conceded meant Westmoor were never boxed in for long. Ethan Hart and Finley Rhodes leaked too many wides early, Caleb Stone had only a short spell, and once the opening stand had already removed the main danger from the chase, every later wicket needed to arrive almost immediately to change the result. That never happened. Westmoor won because they controlled the more important transitions. They did not let Ridgefield’s Caleb-Dylan stand become a full innings. They used Lewis Archer to cut out the middle at exactly the right time. Then Oliver Hayes and Callum Price made the chase small enough that Dylan Foster’s later burst could only make it untidy, not truly uncertain. Lewis Archer was the correct player of the match because his spell gave Westmoor the match’s first real advantage after Ridgefield had begun to recover. Without that control, Ridgefield could have moved from one hundred and two for three into something far more demanding. With it, they were held to one hundred and fifty-two, and even a six-wicket chase with a few late alarms still ended with Westmoor having done enough well before the finish.
