Under a clear but slightly washed-out Eastport sky at Willowbank Oval, the sort of afternoon where the square looks friendlier from the boundary than it feels from the crease, Mariner Vale Dolphins won the toss and sent Cedarbridge Falcons in, and for a little while that call had enough sense behind it to make the fielding side believe they had read the morning correctly, because Ian Mercer found the right length early, not wildly quick, not theatrical, just accurate enough to make the first few overs uncomfortable, Lucas Hart trying to use the new ball’s pace but never quite settling into ownership, Ethan Vale still feeling for the rhythm, and the scoreboard moving in those small, scratchy pieces that make a batting side think about patience before ambition; Hart gave Cedarbridge the first little push before Mercer bowled him, Noah Reed came in and struck one clean boundary before Mercer removed him too, and at 35 for 2 the innings had that delicate shape where nothing is ruined yet but everything can become awkward very quickly if the next stand does not hold. Ethan Vale was the reason it held. He did not turn the match with one reckless burst or some early sequence of slogged boundaries, he turned it by staying calm enough to let the surface reveal itself, first taking the obvious singles, then punching anything too full, then gradually making the Mariner Vale captain move men out of catching positions and into damage control. Mason Cole was the correct partner for that phase, compact, busy, and steady enough to make the partnership feel less like a rescue act and more like a controlled rebuilding operation. Their stand became the first real hinge of the match, carrying Cedarbridge out of early concern and into the sort of middle-over platform from which a big total could be imagined. Mason’s 58 mattered because it gave the innings its first proper support column, and even though Daniel Marsh eventually removed him with the score past 150, the bigger fact was that Vale had already crossed from survival into command. Oliver Hayes then helped keep the innings moving without quite turning it into a surge, and while Victor Hayes trapped him in front, Cedarbridge were no longer in danger of wasting the start; they were now asking how far Vale could carry them. The answer came in the next phase with Ryan Cole, because that stand did not merely add runs, it changed the energy of the innings. Vale began finding the boundary with much greater authority, the checked drive becoming the full drive, the worked single becoming a hard two, and then the lifted hit appearing whenever the length sat up. Ryan Cole’s role was less loud at first, but it was important because he made sure Mariner Vale could not set every plan around Vale. The partnership pushed Cedarbridge toward the last five overs with a heavy total already in sight, and by the time Carter Ross finally removed Vale for 129, the match had shifted from a contest around early wickets to a chase that would require something exceptional. Cole stayed through the finish, Owen Hart’s small burst kept the final overs alive, Liam Brooks sprinted through a quick cameo before being run out, Daniel Page came and went immediately, Adam Shaw helped close the innings, and Cedarbridge finished on 304 for 8 from their 50 overs. The scorecard says the innings was built around Vale, and it was, but not only around him: Mason Cole gave it the repair, Ryan Cole gave it the finish, and the extras column, 26 in all, gave Cedarbridge repeated little releases whenever Mariner Vale seemed close to tightening the game. Mariner Vale’s bowlers had their moments, Ian Mercer striking early, Carter Ross claiming Vale and another late wicket, Daniel Marsh cutting out the big stand, but they never joined those moments into a full squeeze, and that is why the interval already felt heavy. Chasing 305, Mariner Vale needed either a bold opening stand or one player to bat far beyond the rhythm of the innings, and they found neither. Daniel Frost started brightly enough to suggest there might be a little early resistance, but Owen Hart bowled him before the reply had proper shape. Liam Brooks then took over the next section with a controlled spell that mattered more than its drama, removing Kevin Brooks and then Aaron Miles, leaving Mariner Vale three down before the chase had reached even the point where a side can pretend it is simply pacing itself. Henry Lawson became the only batter who offered a genuine middle-order pause, and his 17 was valuable only in the sense that it briefly slowed the collapse rather than challenged the target. Caleb Price removed Oliver Grant, then Lawson, and that was the point where the chase lost its last real thread. Samuel Price tried to stay, Victor Hayes could not turn his start into anything, Benjamin Cole and Ryan Brooks added only short resistance, and every small stand ended before it could become a story. The pressure was not one bowler alone; it came in layers. Hart made the first cut, Liam Brooks damaged the top, Caleb Price removed the only stabilizer, Adam Shaw and Jacob Miles prevented any middle-order repair, and Marcus Doyle made the end look ruthless. Doyle’s late burst gave the scoreline its final sharpness, three wickets for almost nothing, the field tight around the bat, the batters forced into survival strokes when the match had already slipped away. Mariner Vale were all out for 72 in 29.3 overs, and the number tells the story cleanly enough: no batter reached 20, no partnership came close to the size needed for a chase, and the run rate never suggested they were pursuing 305 so much as being slowly compressed by it. Cedarbridge’s win by 232 runs therefore fits the actual cricket rather than exaggerating it. They had early danger, absorbed it through Ethan Vale and Mason Cole, turned control into dominance through Vale and Ryan Cole, and then bowled like a side defending a total large enough to attack without fear. Ethan Vale was the obvious player of the match because his 129 gave Cedarbridge the architecture of the day, but the win was completed by the collective discipline after the break, the kind of second innings where every bowler seemed to understand that the target had already done half the work and their job was simply not to give Mariner Vale a door back into the match.
