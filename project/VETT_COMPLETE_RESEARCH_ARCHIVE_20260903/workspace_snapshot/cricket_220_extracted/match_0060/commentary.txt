Good morning from Northbridge Oval in Harbourside, where Westmere chose to bat first and, for a few overs, looked as though they might build something stubborn enough to make a small-ground match awkward, only for Harbourgate to drag the innings into exactly the kind of low, grinding contest that rewards patience, repeatable lengths and the ability to turn every small batting hesitation into a wicket. The opening passage had that slightly false brightness low-scoring games often carry. There were extras, there was a little movement from Bennett Cross, and Aiden Vale did just enough at the other end to make Westmere believe the new ball had been negotiated rather than survived. But the shape of the innings was never truly free. Connor Shaw’s opening over had been loose, sure, and the extras gave Westmere a cushion they had not fully earned, but once Nathan Cole settled into his line and Logan Wells found his rhythm, the batting began to shrink. Bennett Cross was the first to go, caught by Caleb Brooks off Nathan Cole after making a useful but brief seven, and from there Westmere were pulled into one of those innings where each partnership looks more like a temporary pause than a genuine recovery. Aiden Vale lasted long enough to reach fifteen, two boundaries tucked into a careful stay, but Logan Wells removed him before he could turn the start into the innings’ spine. Carter Shaw and Dylan Reed then tried to rebuild, not with flourish, not with any real authority, but with enough occupation to stop Harbourgate from running straight through the top order. Carter’s eleven was slow and slightly cramped, and when Logan bowled him as well, the innings had slipped into a more fragile shape than the score alone suggested. Dylan Reed and Ethan Holt produced the best stand of the innings in terms of structure, even though it never felt comfortable. Dylan was the anchor, almost stubbornly so, working through long dry sections and refusing to throw away the one set position Westmere still had. Ethan gave him the livelier side of the stand, finding one boundary and keeping the score moving whenever Dylan’s end became too quiet. The partnership carried Westmere into the high eighties and briefly made a total around one-forty imaginable if the lower order could stay with Dylan. That hope lasted only until Dylan Price intervened. He removed Dylan Reed, then Ethan Holt soon after, and suddenly the only meaningful middle-overs repair was gone. Logan Wells then came back for Felix Brooks, and that little sequence, from a workable four-down score into six down almost immediately, was the real break in the innings. Gavin Pierce could not stay. Ryan Grant bowled him. Harvey Cole then became the last source of resistance, and his twenty-two was more important than it looks because it gave Westmere a tail-end shape rather than a complete surrender. He added a few with Isaac Miles, then a few more with Jacob Hayes, and finally dragged the innings with Liam Ward into the one-twenties before Ryan Grant had him caught by Logan Wells. Even that finish had the feel of salvage rather than acceleration. Westmere were all out for one hundred and twenty-four in the forty-third over, a total held together by Dylan’s long stay, Harvey’s late resistance, and a small but important extras column. Harbourgate’s bowling card explained why the innings never opened up. Nathan Cole was hard to score from and took three wickets through disciplined pressure. Ryan Grant’s spell was equally tight and closed out two lower-order pieces. Dylan Price’s two wickets snapped the central stand. Logan Wells, though, was the bowler who kept changing the innings at the exact moments Westmere were searching for calm. His three wickets were not collected after the match was already drifting; they came against Aiden Vale, Carter Shaw and Felix Brooks, three batters positioned at points where Westmere needed continuity. That timing is why the player-of-the-match call fits the shape of the day. Chasing one hundred and twenty-five, Harbourgate should have been comfortable, but small targets at under-nineteen level can become strange things once the first mistake arrives, and Jacob Hayes very nearly turned this one into a theft. Owen Carter never settled and was caught early. Luke Bennett then gave Harbourgate the first clear attacking phase of the chase, punching boundaries with the kind of intent that makes a low target look smaller almost immediately. Mason Hart was quieter beside him, but the stand between those two looked as if it had put the chase back in order. Jacob Hayes refused to let it stay that way. He removed Luke after a sharp twenty-six, then took Nathan Cole, then Caleb Brooks first ball, and all at once Harbourgate had moved from a chase under control into a chase that had four wickets down and more than half the target still requiring proper work. Mason Hart tried to hold the middle together, but his twenty was also ended by Jacob, who completed a five-wicket spell full of persistence, straight lines and enough patience to make every batter start again. The problem for Westmere was that one bowler could not quite carry the whole defence. Evan Blake and Isaac Rowe formed the decisive partnership of the chase, and it was not dramatic in a clean, dominant way; it was valuable because it restored proportion. Evan made twenty-seven, controlled enough to avoid panic and positive enough to make the target keep shrinking. Isaac Rowe gave the stand its sharper pulse, striking a six and keeping the field from crowding the batters completely. Together they took Harbourgate from real discomfort into a position where the finish was close enough for the tail to handle. Benjamin Cross created one final shiver by bowling Evan and then removing Isaac almost immediately, leaving Harbourgate seven down and still needing the last formal steps. But Dylan Price and Ryan Grant did not let the chase become theatre again. They waited, nudged, accepted the wides that came, and finally pushed Harbourgate beyond the target in the thirtieth over. So the match ended as a three-wicket win that felt far more anxious than the first-innings total suggested. Westmere’s innings had been too thin, too dependent on Dylan Reed’s patience and Harvey Cole’s late repair, and too vulnerable once Logan Wells and Dylan Price broke the only central stand. Harbourgate’s chase, meanwhile, was almost derailed by Jacob Hayes’ outstanding five-wicket spell, but they had just enough scattered batting to survive it: Luke Bennett’s early punch, Mason Hart’s slow holding role, then the match-settling work from Evan Blake and Isaac Rowe. Westmere fought hard enough with the ball to make the result look tense, and Jacob Hayes deserved heavy mention for the losing spell, but Harbourgate owned more of the critical phases. Logan Wells was the right player of the match because his spell created the first-innings ceiling Westmere could never break through, and in a game decided by small margins, that ceiling mattered more than any single stroke in the chase.
