At Riverside Park Oval in the Midlands, Ridgegate chose to bat first and built the kind of total that looked powerful at the break and even heavier once Eastmoor started chasing it, because this was not just a score inflated by a few late swings or by one loose over, it was an innings held together by a long, commanding hand from Mason Clarke, sharpened by useful support from Jacob Brown and Ryan Vale, and then dragged just far enough through the final scramble to make the target feel permanently above Eastmoor’s reach. The first few overs still had some contest in them. Dylan Reed gave Ridgegate a bright enough start, taking on the new ball early and making sure the innings did not begin in a crawl, but Eastmoor did get their opening reward when Edward Bell had him stumped with the score at thirty-one. That could have been the moment for the fielding side to slow the innings down. It was not, because Mason Clarke had already begun to read the surface with a clarity nobody else matched. Jacob Brown came in and played the neatest supporting hand of the early middle, four boundaries in a brisk twenty-one, enough to make sure Mason did not have to force every over himself. Their stand of forty-eight gave Ridgegate the platform, and once Jacob was taken by Steven Grant, Ryan Vale arrived and gave the innings its decisive second gear. The partnership between Mason and Ryan was the heart of the match. Ryan’s thirty-two from twenty-four was clean, direct, and sensibly paced, while Mason became more dangerous with every over, first working the ball square, then lifting through the line, then turning anything fractionally short or overpitched into boundary pressure. The eighty-six they added did more than move the total forward. It changed Eastmoor’s bowling rhythm. Timothy Lane, Ronald Stone and Paul Harris all had moments where they seemed close to dragging the rate back, yet the moment one over began to look tidy, Mason found a release shot and made the next field look defensive again. When Timothy finally bowled Ryan at one hundred and sixty-five, Eastmoor had broken the partnership but not the innings. Mason was still there, and although Brian Young later denied him a hundred by removing him for ninety-four, the damage was already deep. Mason’s innings had the right balance of violence and control: eight fours, five sixes, and enough strike management to keep the entire batting effort from turning into one-man chaos. The late overs did get messy. Liam Porter came in and launched one important blow before falling, Carter Mills and Noah Ellis were both removed in the final over sequence, and William Stone had to hold the end together rather than produce a full finish. Still, Ridgegate reached one hundred and eighty-eight for seven, and the shape of the card told the story clearly: the early push from Dylan, the repair-and-rise through Jacob, the major acceleration with Ryan, and Mason’s near-hundred sitting over all of it as the innings that made every smaller contribution matter. Eastmoor’s bowlers collected wickets, but never at the right density. Brian Young’s single over brought two, Paul Harris also took two, and Edward Bell, Steven Grant and Timothy Lane all found a wicket, yet none of them removed Mason early enough to reset the match. Chasing one hundred and eighty-nine, Eastmoor actually began with enough intent to make the first half of the reply feel alive. Kevin Mason attacked straight away, Edward Bell looked dangerous when the ball came into his hitting arc, and the score moved briskly enough to prevent Ridgegate from relaxing. Thomas Taylor removed Kevin, but the second-wicket stand still carried Eastmoor to the high forties before Timothy Lewis was run out, and even then Edward’s striking kept the chase noisy. Jason gave them a useful twenty-two, Steven tried to stretch the innings deeper, and at eighty-five for four the match was not gone, only beginning to narrow. Gary Walker then made it flare again. His thirty from eighteen was the one innings in the chase that truly threatened to bend the ending back toward Eastmoor, and the fifth-wicket stand with Steven was worth forty-six, a passage that pushed the score to one hundred and thirty-one and briefly made Ridgegate look for control rather than simply wait for mistakes. That was the hinge, and Thomas Taylor owned it. He bowled Gary exactly when the chase needed another over of boundary pressure, and once Gary was gone Eastmoor lost the thread almost immediately. Steven followed to Joseph Jackson, Anthony Stone was run out straight after, and Paul Harris could not extend the last real push before Taylor removed him as well. Brian Young and Ronald Stone carried the innings to one hundred and fifty-one, but by then the match had already slipped out of reach. Ridgegate’s bowling was not flawless, especially with a few expensive overs in the middle, but the wickets came at the correct moments. Taylor’s three were the most important because they removed Kevin early, Gary at the peak of the counterattack, and Paul when the tail still had a small mathematical hope. Matthew Thomas took out Edward and Jason, two of the chase’s cleaner scorers. Joseph Jackson got Steven, the one batter trying to hold the innings together. The run-outs tightened the pressure at exactly the point Eastmoor needed calm. So the result, Ridgegate by thirty-seven runs, was a fair reflection of the match’s structure. Eastmoor had enough individual blows to make the chase visible, but not enough continuity to make it truly threatening after Gary’s wicket. Ridgegate had the one innings that towered above the game, the one major partnership that changed the scale of the first innings, and enough timely bowling to stop every Eastmoor recovery just before it became dangerous. Mason Clarke was the correct player of the match because his ninety-four did not merely top-score; it defined the target, dictated the pressure, and forced Eastmoor to chase at a tempo they could only sustain in short bursts.
