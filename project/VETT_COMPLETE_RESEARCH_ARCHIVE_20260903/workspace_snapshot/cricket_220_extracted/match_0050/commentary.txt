At Riverside Academy Ground in Lakeford, Harborline chose to bat first and for more than a quarter of their innings that decision looked close to disastrous, because Reef Bay did not just take early wickets, they made the whole top half of the card feel cramped, uncertain, and repeatedly unfinished, with Fraser Knight striking in the first spell, Henry Cole removing Liam Brooks after a brief burst of boundaries, and Isaac Dunn cutting out Ethan Cole before the Harborline captain could settle into any kind of authority. Noah Reed was the only batter in that first section who looked even partly comfortable, and his 29 mattered because he gave the innings a small working spine while everyone else seemed to be arriving under pressure rather than into rhythm. Logan Hayes and Rhys Turner each stayed long enough to hint at support, but Leo Nash broke both of those little attempts before they became real stands, and when Jack Porter removed Noah Reed with the total only in the mid-eighties and six wickets already gone, Reef Bay had earned the right to feel that the innings was theirs to close. That is exactly where the match changed. Callum Shaw came in with the innings broken and did not try to repair it through one reckless counterattack. He rebuilt it in stages. At first, it was survival with discipline, playing the straight ball late, refusing to chase the wider one, and making sure Mason Pike had enough time to become a genuine partner rather than another name in the collapse. Then the partnership began to breathe. Mason’s 41 from 47 was one of the most important secondary innings in the match, because he did not merely stand beside Callum; he scored often enough to stop Reef Bay from closing the field around one batter, and his boundaries through the off side forced the bowlers to keep defending both ends of the partnership. Their stand of 87 changed the scoreboard and the feeling of the game. Harborline moved from 84 for 6 to 171 for 7, and by the time Mason fell to Jack Porter, the innings was no longer a scramble for respectability. It had become a platform for Callum Shaw to extend. Dylan Cross then played a slow but valuable hand, 11 from 24, not because it lifted the rate by itself, but because it kept Callum in the middle through the next phase and allowed him to transfer the innings from rescue into control. Their partnership added 46, with the extras column again showing that Reef Bay’s early discipline had begun to fray under the pressure of a set batter. Harvey Blake then arrived and gave Callum the final support he needed, making 22 from 24 and helping add 53 for the ninth wicket. That was the stand that truly pushed Harborline into a winning position, because Reef Bay had already taken enough wickets to expect the innings to end around 220 or 230, yet instead they were forced to keep bowling at Callum deep into the last overs. Callum’s 103 from 93 balls was the defining innings of the match: eleven fours, three sixes, and a far more important quality beneath the stroke count, which was his ability to keep changing the meaning of his own innings. He entered as a rescuer, became an anchor, then became the late-innings scorer who made the total heavy. When Fraser Knight finally bowled him at 276, Harborline were all out, but the innings had been transformed from near-collapse into a total Reef Bay would have to chase with sustained discipline. Reef Bay’s bowling deserved credit for the early damage and for continuing to strike, especially Fraser Knight’s three wickets, Jack Porter’s two important middle wickets, Leo Nash’s pressure burst, and Isaac Dunn’s tidy early work, but the cost of failing to finish the innings after 84 for 6 was severe. The wides, the no-balls, and the inability to keep Callum isolated turned a dominant position into a chase of 277. Reef Bay’s reply began cautiously rather than explosively, and that was already a problem because a target of that size needed either one commanding top-order hand or two extended stands. Ben Carter was bowled by Harvey Blake before the opening pair had created a base, and although Aaron Holt and Callum Price briefly steadied the innings, Mason Pike removed Aaron before the chase had enough tempo to feel threatening. The only passage where Reef Bay looked capable of constructing something substantial came through Daniel Fox and Callum Price. Their stand of 39 was not fast, but it was the one period where the innings had shape: Callum Price finding a few boundaries, Daniel Fox occupying one end, and Harborline needing to work rather than simply wait for errors. Callum Shaw then stepped in again, this time with the ball, trapping Callum Price lbw and removing the one batter who had begun to give the reply a proper route. Ethan Cole followed by bowling Elliot Marsh, and that wicket mattered because the Reef Bay captain never got the chance to become the middle-order organizer the chase needed. Daniel Fox kept batting, but the innings around him kept narrowing. Fraser Knight stayed for a while without changing the pressure. George Miles could not build. Henry Cole struck a couple of brief blows but did not last. Isaac Dunn, Jack Porter, and the lower order were forced into survival rather than a chase. Harvey Blake was the bowler who turned that pressure into a finished result. His 5 for 31 was spread across the innings in exactly the right way: Ben Carter early, Daniel Fox when the chase needed its last stable batter, Fraser Knight before he could build a second center, then Henry Cole and Jack Porter to prevent the tail from stretching the match. Mason Pike’s two wickets gave him an important all-round match, and Callum Shaw’s two wickets completed the logic of his player-of-the-match award, because he had already rescued Harborline with the bat and then removed the batter most capable of keeping Reef Bay’s reply coherent. Dylan Cross’s spell was also quietly brutal, four overs, two maidens, only five runs, and no release for a batting side that needed steady scoring almost every over. Reef Bay were eventually bowled out for 113, and the 163-run margin was a fair reflection of how completely Harborline won the decisive passages. Reef Bay won the early first-innings phase, but Harborline won everything that mattered after that: the rescue through Callum and Mason, the late lift through Callum and Harvey, the middle-innings break in the chase, and the lower-order dismantling. Callum Shaw was the correct player of the match because his hundred changed a near-lost batting innings into a commanding total, and his spell then helped ensure Reef Bay never converted their one usable partnership into anything resembling a chase.
