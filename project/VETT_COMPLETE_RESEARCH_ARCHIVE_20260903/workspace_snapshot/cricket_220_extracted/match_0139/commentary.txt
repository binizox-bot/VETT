At Oakridge Oval in Fairhaven, with the match on the line, Stonegate XI won the toss and chose to field first, and for a few overs that decision did not look foolish so much as dangerous, because Harborview XI did not blast the final open immediately in a wild rush, yet they very quickly began doing something more useful in a title match: they made Stonegate bowl under pressure without ever giving them the comfort of a clear squeeze. Elias Rowe and Rohan Vale gave Harborview a base that was not just fast enough but stable enough, Elias playing the useful early hand with thirty-one from twenty-four, three boundaries and one lifted shot over the rope, while Rohan, the captain, started with a little more patience and then gradually took control of the whole innings. The first stand reached eighty-two, helped by a few extras, and what mattered about that partnership was the way it denied Stonegate the first breakthrough until almost half the innings had gone. Noah Vale eventually created the opening by having Elias caught, but by then Harborview had already turned the toss decision into a burden rather than an advantage. Arman Cole came in and added exactly the kind of second-phase support a set captain needs, twenty from sixteen, enough rotation, enough boundary threat, and enough calm that Rohan did not have to rebuild after the wicket. Mason Reed removed Arman with the score at one hundred and twenty-four, and that should have been Stonegate’s chance to drag the innings into a quieter middle-late phase, but Rohan simply shifted gears. Dane Mercer joined him and the innings became sharper almost immediately, not just because Dane hit thirty-seven not out from eighteen at the back end, but because Rohan used that phase to turn a strong captain’s innings into the defining hand of the final. He reached ninety-four from fifty-five, struck seven fours and six sixes, and the innings was powerful because it was not trapped in one gear. He gave shape to the opening, controlled the middle, then attacked hard enough with Dane to push Harborview toward a score that felt like match territory. Carter Flynn finally removed Rohan at one hundred and seventy-five, and then bowled Jonah Mills shortly after, while Theo Grant was run out in the final over, so Stonegate did pick up late wickets, but those dismissals were not enough to alter the weight of the innings. Dane stayed unbeaten, Nico Bell watched the last ball from the other end, and Harborview closed on one hundred and ninety-six for five, a total built on one huge captain’s innings, one proper opening base, and one brisk finishing hand. Stonegate’s bowling card showed effort but not enough control. Carter Flynn took two wickets, Noah Vale and Mason Reed each found one, and Milo Grant’s fielding produced the late run-out, yet too many overs were lived on Harborview’s terms, especially once Rohan had settled and Dane began striking cleanly at the death. Chasing one hundred and ninety-seven in a final demanded a calm start and then a serious middle-order hand, but Julian Frost made sure Stonegate got neither. He struck almost immediately, bowling Noah Vale before the chase had found a rhythm, then removed Milo Grant and Carter Flynn in the same opening spell, and when Mason Reed fell to Arman Cole with the score still stuck in the teens, Stonegate were nineteen for four and already trying to repair a chase that had barely begun. That opening burst was the moment the final tilted decisively toward Harborview. Leo Price and Finn Brooks did their best to bring some order back, and their stand of thirty-four had genuine value because it stopped the reply from collapsing outright. Leo’s fourteen was more survival than command, while Finn’s twenty-seven offered the first real sign of counterplay, but Arman Cole broke that stand before it could become a platform, and Kieran Holt later had Finn stumped to leave Stonegate six down for sixty-five. Owen Lane was the one batter who refused to let the chase become a procession. His forty-four from thirty balls had four fours and two sixes, and for a while he made the innings look less damaged than it really was, first stitching together a small recovery with Jude Carter, then adding useful runs with Isaac Stone and Caleb York. But the problem for Stonegate was that every partnership around Owen felt like a patch rather than a structure. Jude Carter fell to Jonah Mills, Isaac Stone was bowled by the same bowler, and although Caleb York struck seventeen not out and Tariq Bell finished with a sharp thirteen from three balls, the chase had already lost too much ground in the first four overs to become genuinely dangerous. Owen’s run-out at one hundred and thirty-four for nine ended the last real thread of resistance, and Stonegate eventually reached one hundred and forty-seven for nine, forty-nine short. Harborview’s defense was complete in the way finals often demand. Julian Frost broke the top order and changed the whole emotional shape of the chase. Arman Cole removed the next two batters who might have stabilized it. Kieran Holt cut out Finn Brooks before the middle order could settle. Jonah Mills controlled the late middle with two wickets. Nico Bell’s run-out of Owen Lane made sure the last resistance did not turn into a cosmetic surge. The result therefore felt fully earned. Stonegate had small moments: Flynn’s two wickets, Owen Lane’s counterpunch, Caleb York and Tariq Bell’s late hitting. But Harborview had the innings that mattered, the spell that mattered, and the cleaner control of the match’s decisive sections. Rohan Vale was the correct player of the match because his ninety-four did not simply top-score; it gave Harborview the final’s shape, held three partnerships together, and created a total Stonegate could only chase by playing a near-perfect second innings. They never got close to that. Harborview batted with structure, defended with early violence, and closed the match as deserved winners.
