At Lakeside Cricket Ground in Eastport, with match sitting over the whole afternoon and Westport choosing to field first, Ridgefield’s innings became a long exercise in repair rather than dominance, because they were never allowed to settle for long, never given the kind of unbroken top-order platform that lets a side cruise toward a total, and yet they kept finding one more partnership, one more hand, one more awkward little stand to push the score beyond what Westport probably thought they were allowing. Ethan Reed and Liam Parker began carefully but not passively, Ethan the cleaner early scorer with three boundaries in his 24, Liam taking longer to settle and looking more concerned with occupation than tempo. The opening stand reached the low thirties before Owen Grant had Ethan caught, and that wicket gave Westport exactly the first interruption they wanted. Liam and Noah Grant then built the most substantial early passage of the innings, not a fluent stand in the sense of boundaries everywhere, but a proper second-wicket recovery, Noah’s 20 from 30 giving Liam the support he needed while the ball softened and Westport’s attack had to move from new-ball threat into control mode. That stand carried Ridgefield to 80, and for a moment the innings looked as though it might finally have a stable road ahead. It did not. Nathan Brooks removed Noah, Connor Reed then took Liam, and suddenly what had looked like a usable platform had become three down with the innings still well short of safety. This is where Owen Clarke’s role became central. He did not arrive with the luxury of an already-built base. He came in while the innings was still being pulled apart in pieces, and his 68 from 82 was valuable because it solved several different problems in sequence. First he had to stop the slide after Liam’s dismissal. Then he had to work with Caleb Frost, who made only 7 but helped carry the score through one small reset. Then he had to manage the partnership with Logan Price, whose 14 from 24 and one six gave Ridgefield just enough release to keep Westport from squeezing every over into dots. Owen Grant bowled Logan and Parker Lane quickly removed Mason Reid, so Ridgefield were six down with the innings still fragile, yet Owen Clarke kept refusing to let the score freeze. Aiden Ross gave him a short push, Connor Hayes then played the most important lower-order support hand with 18 from 24, and their eighth-wicket stand was the passage that changed Ridgefield’s innings from a possible sub-180 scramble into a defendable total. Connor’s three boundaries mattered because they stopped Westport from parking the field around Owen Clarke alone, and Owen, by then fully into the rhythm of the pitch, kept collecting in a way that looked unspectacular ball by ball and increasingly important over by over. When Connor was run out at 190, Ridgefield still had work to do, and Dylan Brooks gave them the final proper spark, his 16 from 13 with a boundary and a six adding late bite before Evan Ross removed him. Owen Clarke dragged the innings into the last over with Parker Grant beside him, and when Logan Hayes finally removed the captain at 224, the value of the innings was obvious. Ridgefield had not posted a huge score, but they had made Westport bat properly. The total had been built from resistance: 31 at the top, then a better second stand, then fragments, then Owen Clarke repeatedly stitching those fragments into something coherent. Westport’s bowling was good enough to keep taking wickets. Parker Lane was tidy and effective, Connor Reed broke the middle, Owen Grant hit both early and mid-innings, Nathan Brooks was economical, and Logan Hayes closed the innings by taking the big wicket. Yet the fielding side never turned a breakthrough into a full collapse, and that failure mattered once the chase began. Westport’s reply opened with such authority that Ridgefield’s 224 immediately began to look vulnerable. Aaron Blake and Blake Morgan played the first dozen overs as if they had understood the pitch better than anyone else, Aaron especially crisp through the off side and quick to punish anything too full, while Blake Morgan gave the partnership its balance, calmer but still scoring cleanly enough that the chase never felt stuck. Their opening stand of 74 came almost entirely from proper batting rather than gifts, and that detail shaped the early feeling of the chase. Ridgefield were not losing control through wides or panic. They were simply being outplayed by two openers who had started well. Connor Hayes changed the match by bowling Aaron Blake, and from that moment the chase began to tilt, gradually at first, then with much more force. Carter Hayes arrived and never looked settled, even though the stand with Blake Morgan pushed Westport close to three figures. Caleb Frost then removed Blake Morgan, and that was the first truly major wound, because the set batter had been the one holding the chase’s structure together after Aaron’s departure. Connor Hayes soon trapped Carter, and suddenly Westport had gone from a strong opening to three down with the middle order asked to restart under pressure. Nathan Brooks and Declan Ward tried to build the next phase, Nathan’s 21 from 25 briefly restoring some tempo, Declan playing more slowly and looking to anchor the innings, but Liam Parker cut out Nathan at 130, and that wicket exposed the difference between Westport’s first hour and everything after it. The openers had created momentum; the middle order could not sustain it. Evan Ross flashed one boundary and fell to Parker Grant. Mason Cooper became Caleb Frost’s second victim. Parker Lane’s 10 from 12 gave a short late flicker, but Caleb removed him too, Owen Clarke taking the catch, and once Connor Reed fell first ball to the same bowler, Westport’s chase had been stripped down to Declan Ward and the tail. Declan’s 24 from 50 was an effort to hold the card together, but by the time he was caught off Caleb Frost the match had already changed shape completely. He had survived; he had not found enough scoring support to make survival dangerous. Logan Hayes struck one boundary at the end, Owen Grant remained not out, and Connor Hayes finished the innings by removing Logan at 171. The decisive difference in the second innings was pressure discipline. Ridgefield gave away very little for free, and that made every lost wicket heavier. Caleb Frost’s five wickets were the obvious bowling headline, and the spell was devastating because of when the wickets came: the set Blake Morgan, then Mason Cooper, Parker Lane, Connor Reed, and Declan Ward. He did not merely clean up after the match was already finished; he removed the pieces Westport still needed to keep the chase alive. Connor Hayes was just as important structurally, taking Aaron Blake, Carter Hayes, and the final wicket, giving Ridgefield the first cut, the middle cut, and the close. Liam Parker’s wicket of Nathan Brooks mattered because Nathan was the one batter after the opening stand who briefly threatened to rebuild the innings with authority. Parker Grant’s dismissal of Evan Ross kept the lower middle from finding sudden acceleration. So the match turned on sequence rather than one isolated moment. Ridgefield’s 224 looked moderate until Owen Clarke’s innings was placed against the pressure of a chase that received almost nothing cheaply and then collapsed after a strong opening. Westport were ahead at 74 without loss and still well placed close to the hundred mark, but they never found another partnership with the same clarity. Ridgefield, by contrast, were never dominant with the bat, yet they were stubborn enough to keep the innings alive through Owen Clarke, then precise enough with the ball to make that stubborn total feel much bigger. That is why Owen Clarke was the right player of the match. Caleb Frost’s five-wicket spell was spectacular and nearly good enough for the award on its own, but Owen Clarke’s 68 was the innings that created the contest Ridgefield later defended. Without that long captain’s hand, there would not have been enough scoreboard pressure for the bowling to exploit. With it, Westport’s early 74-run opening stand became only a temporary lead in a match Ridgefield eventually controlled through patience, pressure, and better timing.
