At Windshore Cricket Academy Ground in Marwick, Ridgefront A won the toss, chose to bat, and at first that decision looked fragile rather than commanding, because Seabourne Strikers opened with exactly the kind of early discipline that can make a first innings feel cramped before it has even settled. Cole Hart found the first break by bowling Arden Wells at thirteen, and before the fourth over had properly opened he had pinned Eli Cole as well, leaving Ridgefront at sixteen for two and forcing the innings into repair mode almost immediately. That early phase matters because Ridgefront did not build their total from a clean launch; they had to rebuild it from damage. Cory Blake and Mason Reid were the pair who changed the tone. Cory’s twenty-seven from twenty-three was measured more than spectacular, three boundaries and a lot of useful occupation, while Mason became the more substantial hand, fifty-one from thirty-nine, three fours and three sixes, and the batter who made Seabourne pay whenever they drifted out of the tight channel Leo Carter and Tyler Quinn were trying to hold. Their stand of sixty-six was the structural center of the innings because it took Ridgefront from early trouble into a position where the back half could still be attacked. Marcus Vale ended Cory’s stay with a caught-and-bowled dismissal, and Leo Carter later bowled Mason at one hundred and nine, so Seabourne had not lost the match completely through the middle. In fact, when Dylan Cross also fell at one hundred and twenty-four, caught by Marcus off Cameron Holt, the innings was still in a very negotiable place. A score around the mid-one-sixties was possible, perhaps even likely, and Seabourne had enough reason to think the early wickets would still matter. Then Kian Mercer and Luke Parnell took the last phase away from them. Kian’s unbeaten thirty-nine from eighteen was the match’s decisive batting burst, not just because it was fast, but because it arrived at the exact point where Ridgefront were five down and vulnerable to a stalled finish. He hit cleanly through the line, did not waste the over when the field spread, and made every small miss at the death feel expensive. Luke Parnell’s unbeaten twenty from eleven was the ideal support act, a boundary, two sixes, and no wasted sense of caution. Their unbroken stand of fifty-five lifted Ridgefront to one hundred and seventy-nine for five, and that final total looked much heavier than the innings had promised at sixteen for two or even at one hundred and twenty-four for five. Seabourne’s bowling had good work inside it, especially Cole Hart’s early two wickets and Leo Carter’s four overs for only twenty, but the attack leaked badly around those spells, and the nine wides plus a no-ball only deepened the sense that the pressure had broken their execution late. Chasing one hundred and eighty, Seabourne needed a brisk, confident opening and then one batter to carry the middle. They got pieces of the first requirement but never the full version. Tyler Quinn and Logan Pierce put on thirty-one, yet it took nearly six overs and never really forced Ridgefront to defend anxiously. Mason Reid trapped Tyler in front, Luke Parnell removed Evan Brook soon after, and when Isaac Shaw bowled Logan at fifty-six in the eleventh over, the chase had already moved into a dangerous kind of half-life: not dead, not collapsing, but too slow for the size of the target. Cameron Holt and Nathan Cole then produced the best partnership of the reply, adding thirty-five, but even that stand was more about survival than acceleration. Cameron made thirty from thirty-one, Nathan thirteen from twenty-one, and by the time Ryan Price removed Nathan at ninety-one the required rate had already climbed beyond the shape of the innings. Owen Hayes then made sure there would be no late illusion of a comeback, bowling Cameron and later removing Blake Turner, while Caleb Foster cut off Jordan Hayes before the last over could become anything more than score correction. Marcus Vale struck one late six and Leo Carter stayed with him, but those final runs only softened the look of the card rather than changing the match. Seabourne finished on one hundred and eleven for seven, and the reason the margin stretched to sixty-eight was not a single dramatic collapse; it was the absence of any stand that truly changed the pace of the chase. Ridgefront’s bowlers shared the control well. Ryan Price was miserly and removed Nathan, Isaac Shaw’s spell squeezed the top order and took Logan, Luke Parnell struck at the right time, Mason Reid made the first breakthrough in the chase, and Owen Hayes gave the late overs their final shape with two wickets. When the game is reduced to its basic structure, Seabourne won the first few overs of the match, Ridgefront won the middle through Cory and Mason, then won the finish emphatically through Kian and Luke, and after that Seabourne never produced an equivalent answer with the bat. That is why Kian Mercer was the right player of the match. Mason Reid made the larger score, but Kian’s unbeaten hand was the innings that changed the final score from competitive to overwhelming, and once Seabourne let that final stand get away, the chase had almost no margin for a slow middle. Ridgefront A won by sixty-eight runs because they recovered better, finished harder, and then defended with far more consistent pressure than Seabourne could absorb.
