At Westhaven Training Oval in Northshore, with the match sitting heavy over every decision, Ridgeport A chose to bat first and almost immediately found themselves dragged into the kind of disaster that turns a final from a planned innings into a rescue operation, because Highland Bay A did not simply take early wickets, they took them in a way that stripped the batting side of rhythm, shape and even basic tempo before the fifth over had closed. Aron Field was the first to go, pushing at Carter Blake and sending the catch to Ben Howe with only three on the board, and that early wicket mattered because it gave Highland permission to attack rather than contain. Mason Vale tried to settle the innings but never really looked comfortable, and Nolan Carter had him stumped before the powerplay had found any shape. Elias Stone followed soon after when Carter Blake hit the stumps again, and Connor Hayes was bowled by Nolan Carter before he could build any kind of repair. At eighteen for four, Ridgeport were not merely behind the game. They were almost outside it. That is why Ryan Dell’s innings became so important. He did not walk in with the luxury of a platform or the permission to swing freely. He walked in with the match already broken and had to make a final look like a contest again. Luca Price was the first partner to give him anything substantial, and their stand of fifty was the first point in the innings where Ridgeport began to breathe. Luca’s twenty-one from seventeen gave the score enough movement to stop Highland from setting ultra-attacking fields every ball, while Ryan took the longer role, judging when to absorb, when to nudge, and when to finally take the boundary option. Even so, Highland kept finding ways to prevent the rescue from becoming a takeover. Luca was run out at sixty-eight, and that ended the first serious rebuild before it could become a full launch. Dylan Ward then gave Ryan the next vital hand, making twenty-two from seventeen and adding forty-one with him, a partnership that carried Ridgeport into the final quarter with at least a possible defendable score in view. But again the damage came through interruption. Dylan was run out by Liam Fox, Owen Frost was bowled first ball by Carter Blake, and Ridgeport were suddenly seven down with the death overs still requiring improvisation. Ryan Dell handled that last section with admirable calm, finishing unbeaten on sixty-six from forty-eight, and the last stand with Noah Grant added twenty-seven, enough to lift Ridgeport to one hundred and thirty-seven for seven. In isolation, that total looked thin for a final. In context, after eighteen for four, it was a serious salvage. Highland Bay’s bowling card explains the innings clearly: Carter Blake’s three wickets gave the attack its edge, Nolan Carter’s two early strikes tore through the top order, Evan Scott and Tyler Quinn kept enough pressure around the wickets, and even when Aidan Ford and Liam Carter leaked runs later, the early damage had already given Highland a cushion. Ridgeport had survived through Ryan, not imposed through the batting unit. Then the chase began with a shock of its own, because Ryan Dell, fresh from carrying the first innings, bowled Nate Shaw first ball and gave Ridgeport exactly the opening they needed if one hundred and thirty-seven was going to grow into something psychologically larger. For a few minutes, the final had that nervous low-target feel. Marcus Vale, though, read the situation with exceptional discipline. He did not try to answer the first-ball wicket with a rush of ego. He let Jason Brooks play the more active role in the first rebuilding stand, and Jason’s twenty-five from twenty-one did exactly what Highland needed: it took the chase from shock into structure. Their stand of forty-five was not spectacular, but it was deeply useful because it prevented Ridgeport from turning one wicket into a cluster. Noah Grant removed Jason with Mason Vale taking the catch, and that was Ridgeport’s best chance to squeeze the chase again, but Marcus stayed settled. Liam Carter then joined him and supplied the most important support hand of the chase, thirty-four from twenty-seven, not through a long list of fours but through clean, well-timed six-hitting that changed the rhythm of the innings whenever Ridgeport started to believe they had slowed it down. The Marcus-Liam stand of fifty-five moved Highland from forty-five for two to one hundred for three, and that was the phase where the result began to shift decisively. Marcus did the less noisy but more central work: keeping the chase proportionate, not allowing dot balls to stack into panic, and making sure the target was reduced in controlled pieces rather than chased in a single reckless burst. Logan Pierce did bowl Liam at one hundred, and for a brief moment Ridgeport could still imagine a final twist if Marcus went quickly. But Ben Foster arrived and ended that thought with the right finishing energy. His unbeaten fifteen from five balls gave the last phase a clean release, while Marcus completed the chase on fifty-seven not out from fifty-five, an innings that looks modest in strike rate until you remember that he came in at none for one, absorbed every change of pressure, and never let Ridgeport’s low total become a trap. Highland Bay reached one hundred and thirty-eight for three in eighteen overs and one ball, winning by seven wickets, and the result felt accurate because they had owned the decisive parts of both innings. Ridgeport’s Ryan Dell was outstanding in defeat, first saving the innings and then striking first ball with the ball, but Highland had the more complete final performance: Carter Blake and Nolan Carter shattered the top order, the fielding created two run-outs when Ridgeport were repairing, Marcus Vale controlled the chase, Liam Carter gave the middle phase its momentum, and Ben Foster finished before the match could become tense. That is why Marcus Vale was the correct player of the match. His unbeaten fifty-seven did not need to be the loudest innings of the day. It needed to be the innings that carried the title chase from danger into certainty, and that is exactly what it did.
