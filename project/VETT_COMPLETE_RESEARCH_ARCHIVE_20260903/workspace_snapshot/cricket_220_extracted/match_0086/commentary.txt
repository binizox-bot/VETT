At Central Academy Ground in Sandhurst, Aquila chose to field in this semi-final and for quite a long time that decision looked sensible, even almost sharp, because Dune were never allowed to sprint away from the game and never had that clean top-order spell where a knockout innings suddenly becomes untouchable. The important thing, though, was that Dune kept finding just enough resistance at exactly the moments where Aquila needed the next wicket to become a collapse. Ben Turner went early, bowled by Tyler Brooks after a brief opening stand that had barely begun to form, and that gave Aquila the first little grip on the match. Arlen Quinn then became the early stabilizer, not flashy, not trying to turn the powerplay into a statement, just strong enough through the off side and patient enough against the straighter ball to keep the innings from folding inward. His stand with Carter Wells pushed Dune out of the first danger zone, and although a handful of extras helped the score move, Carter’s fifteen and Arlen’s calm scoring made the innings feel less brittle than the wicket column suggested. Ryan Cross broke that stand, Connor Shaw later removed Gavin Scott, and when Liam Hayes bowled Elliot Hays for a duck, Dune were 79 for 4 and the whole innings was sitting on a narrow ledge. Aquila had created the exact sort of semi-final pressure they wanted: no runaway start, the top order partly opened, the scoring rate still modest, and the batting side needing to rebuild rather than expand. Arlen Quinn tried to hold that rebuild together a little longer with Nathan Cole, but Tyler Brooks returned to bowl him for forty-four, and at 94 for 5 Dune looked less like a side setting a target and more like one trying to find any total with teeth. That was the moment Jordon Pierce entered the game fully. His partnership with Nathan Cole became the decisive passage of the first innings, not because it was enormous in isolation, but because it changed the meaning of the score. The sixth-wicket stand of fifty-eight was built mostly from the bat, which matters here, because it was not a rescue inflated by wides or fielding-side panic. Nathan Cole did the patient work, taking the time the situation demanded and giving Jordon enough security to avoid playing as if every ball had to be forced. Jordon, meanwhile, gave Dune the one innings that had both survival and forward movement inside it. He made fifty-seven from fifty-five balls, and the value of that hand was in its timing: he arrived when the innings was half-broken, took it past the immediate danger, then kept stretching the total after Nathan fell. Caleb Foster eventually removed Nathan at 152, and Aquila still had a route to keep the score near the 170s if they could rush the tail. They partly did that. Isaac Lane was bowled by Liam Hayes, Evan Brooks was pinned by Caleb Foster, and Cole Mercer did not get far before Tyler Brooks struck again. Yet Jordon kept adding little pieces, first with Isaac, then with Evan, then with Cole, and finally with Blake Hart in a last partnership that carried the total beyond 200 before Tyler Brooks ended the innings. Dune’s 201 all out was not a dominant batting return, but it was a hard, inconvenient total built from a dangerous 94 for 5. Aquila’s bowlers had strong claims across the card: Tyler Brooks took four and gave the innings its frame, Caleb Foster’s two wickets came with excellent control, Liam Hayes was tidy and struck twice, Connor Shaw removed a useful middle-order hand, and Ryan Cross helped break the early repair. The slight frustration for Aquila was that their pressure did not become terminal at 94 for 5. Jordon Pierce and Nathan Cole made sure of that, and those extra thirty or forty runs after the first collapse would become the match. Chasing 202, Aquila began about as badly as a side can begin, Ethan Cole bowled by Blake Hart before the innings had a run beside it. Luca Reed and Mason Blake patched the first wound, but the stand was only a first repair, not a foundation, and when Evan Brooks had Luca stumped at 24, Dune were still very much in the contest. Then came the partnership that should have taken Aquila into the final. Noah Grant and Mason Blake added eighty-five for the third wicket without a single extra, and that detail is the strongest evidence of how well they were batting during that spell. Noah was the cleaner scorer, reaching fifty-two from fifty-four with four boundaries and one six, and Mason was the structural partner, absorbing the quiet overs, placing the ball into gaps, and making sure the chase did not become a series of nervous starts. From 24 for 2 to 109 for 3, Aquila had not merely recovered; they had taken control of the pursuit. The target was manageable, the required rate was not frightening, and two batters had solved enough of the surface to make the dressing room believe the hard part had been handled. Then the semi-final turned. Isaac Lane trapped Noah lbw at 109, and before Aquila could reset, Mason Blake was run out at 112. That double break changed the entire mood of the chase. Owen Parker was bowled by Evan Brooks soon after, Dylan Price was run out just as the innings needed calm, and Liam Hayes was also run out with the score still stuck at 130. The collapse from 109 for 3 to 130 for 7 is the match’s central wound. It was not only a loss of wickets, it was a loss of order. One set batter removed by bowling pressure, one set partner lost in the field, then the middle order handing back control through broken running and poor judgment. Dune did not need to blast through Aquila in one spell; Aquila began doing some of the damage themselves. Caleb Foster and Ryan Cross still made the game tense, and that is important because the chase never died immediately. Their eighth-wicket stand of forty-one dragged Aquila from the edge of defeat into a position where Dune had to keep thinking. Ryan Cross made twenty-five and supplied the only real late boundary threat, while Caleb Foster played the long, difficult holding role, finishing unbeaten on seventeen after spending most of the last twenty overs trying to keep one end intact. Extras helped that stand too, and that was both useful and revealing: Aquila were moving, but not always through enough clean batting to fully erase the earlier collapse. Gavin Scott removed Ryan at 171, and that wicket was nearly decisive because it took away the one lower-order player who could still change the chase quickly. Connor Shaw gave Aquila one last surge, hitting a six and pushing the score to 189, close enough for the result to feel genuinely alive again. Cole Mercer then delivered the final act with a calmness that matched the best of Dune’s defence. He had Connor caught behind, then bowled Tyler Brooks two balls later, leaving Caleb Foster stranded and Aquila all out for 190 with five balls still unused. That ending fit the whole match: Aquila had enough skill to keep re-entering the contest, but not enough composure to finish the chase they had once controlled. Dune’s defence was not built on one spectacular spell. Blake Hart gave them the perfect start. Evan Brooks removed Luca and Owen to keep the middle exposed. Isaac Lane took the most important wicket of the chase by dismissing Noah Grant. Gavin Scott broke the Ryan Cross-Caleb Foster stand. Cole Mercer closed the match when the pressure was at its sharpest. Jordon Pierce, even without taking a wicket, bowled ten overs of disciplined control after making the first-innings fifty that lifted Dune from danger, and that is why his player-of-the-match award is justified. He was the player who gave 201 meaning, then helped defend that meaning while Aquila’s chase lost its structure. The final margin of eleven runs is exactly the right size for this game: narrow enough to show how close Aquila came, large enough to show how costly their middle collapse was, and clear enough to prove that Dune won the semi-final through resilience, timing, and composure under pressure.
