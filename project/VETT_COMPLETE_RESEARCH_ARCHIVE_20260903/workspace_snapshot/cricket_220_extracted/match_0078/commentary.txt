At Central Academy Oval in Silvermere, Westhaven won the toss, chose to field, and spent the first innings taking wickets often enough to prevent Coastline from ever enjoying a completely loose afternoon, yet not often enough to stop the innings from gathering weight in three separate waves, first through the early order giving the innings movement, then through Ryan Vale and Dylan Hayes turning a slightly nervous start into a real platform, and finally through Lucas Bennett transforming a useful total into something that looked genuinely defendable at the break. Coastline’s opening was brisk without being reckless. Kieran Moss was the sharper presence early, driving and cutting his way to twenty-seven, while Ryan Vale took the longer route beside him, and the opening stand of forty-five did the important first job of making Westhaven wait for control after choosing to bowl. Blake Carter eventually removed Kieran, and when Mason Reed also went shortly afterward, caught behind off Tyler Ward, Coastline had slipped into the first awkward pocket of the innings. At fifty-four for two, Westhaven had a chance to turn the morning into a squeeze. Ryan Vale and Dylan Hayes denied them that. Their stand of eighty-eight was the hinge of Coastline’s innings, not because it was explosive from start to finish, but because it restored order and then kept stretching the fielding side until the middle overs became increasingly expensive in time and patience. Ryan’s sixty-four from seventy-nine had enough authority to make the bowlers keep adjusting, while Dylan’s fifty-seven from ninety-four was slower and more adhesive, the kind of captain’s innings that keeps an innings intact while others change tempo around it. Evan Clarke finally removed Ryan at one hundred and forty-two, and Westhaven did well enough in the next few overs to prevent a clean acceleration. Ethan Cross did not last, Noah Parker’s little burst was cut off by Blake Carter, and for a moment at one hundred and seventy-four for five the innings still looked like it might settle somewhere competitive rather than commanding. Lucas Bennett changed that reading. His forty-nine from forty balls gave Coastline the late force the innings had been waiting for, and his partnership with Dylan Hayes was the second important shift after the big Ryan-Dylan stand. Dylan fell at two hundred and nineteen, but Lucas kept finding the rope and clearing it when Westhaven missed their length. Owen Foster, Caleb Turner and Liam Grant all helped in smaller pieces, and although Tyler Ward’s four-wicket spell kept breaking the lower order, he could not stop the total from moving past the mid-two-sixties. By the end Coastline had reached two hundred and sixty-seven for nine, a score built less on one overwhelming individual innings than on a layered batting card: Ryan Vale anchoring the first half, Dylan Hayes carrying the middle, Lucas Bennett pushing the final phase, and enough extras from Westhaven to stop pressure from becoming a full clamp. Westhaven’s bowling was useful in fragments. Tyler Ward was the best wicket-taker and deserved credit for removing Mason Reed, Ethan Cross, Caleb Turner and Lucas Bennett. Blake Carter’s two wickets gave them early and middle-overs value. Cameron Scott took two of his own, including Dylan Hayes and Owen Foster. Evan Clarke’s wicket of Ryan Vale was the biggest breakthrough before the death overs. Yet the collective discipline never stayed tight long enough, and the extras column meant Coastline kept being nudged forward even in overs where the batters were being made to work. Chasing two hundred and sixty-eight, Westhaven began with enough uncertainty to keep the match alive. Zane Morris made only ten before Owen Foster had him caught by Ryan Vale, and at thirty-four for one Coastline had the first opening they needed if they were going to make the target feel heavy. Connor Price and Adrian Wells then produced the stand that changed the entire chase. Connor was the more obviously forceful partner in the first half of the partnership, reaching sixty-seven with five fours and three sixes, while Adrian Wells played the innings around which everything else eventually settled. Their second-wicket stand of one hundred and nineteen took Westhaven from early danger to full control, and it had the kind of calm shape a chase requires: no panic after the wicket, no unnecessary acceleration, no long dead spell that allowed Coastline to crowd the bat. When Owen Foster removed Connor at one hundred and fifty-three, there was still enough left in the chase for Coastline to imagine a late squeeze if Logan Shaw needed time to settle. Logan gave them no such opening. He arrived with a finishing tempo that completely changed the final third of the pursuit, making sixty-eight not out from forty-seven balls and forcing the field back almost immediately. Adrian, meanwhile, moved from set batter to match-winner without changing the basic logic of his innings. His unbeaten hundred and eight from one hundred and twenty-five balls was the defining performance of the day because he was present through every meaningful phase of the chase: after the first wicket, through Connor Price’s long support, after the second wicket, and then to the finish beside Logan Shaw. The unbroken stand of one hundred and eighteen made the result feel emphatic rather than merely controlled. Logan supplied the clean acceleration, Adrian supplied the continuity, and Coastline’s bowlers were left needing an error that never arrived. Owen Foster was the only bowler to break through, and although Caleb Turner and Dylan Hayes produced some steadier overs, the attack as a whole could not sustain pressure. Aiden Brooks gave away too much width early, Ethan Cross was expensive late, and the chase kept slipping into Westhaven’s preferred rhythm. That is why the final score of two hundred and seventy-one for two felt so decisive. Coastline had made a strong total and had batted in sensible phases, but Westhaven answered with the superior chase structure: one early loss, one huge second-wicket stand, then an unbroken finish. Adrian Wells was the correct player of the match because his hundred did not merely decorate the chase; it organized it. By the close, Westhaven had made a demanding target look manageable, then manageable look routine, and that is why their eight-wicket win felt earned rather than gifted.
