At Northbank Meadow Ground in Hillview, Gulfstone XI won the toss and chose to field, and for a short while that call looked sharp enough to dictate the whole afternoon, because Summit Vale XI were pushed almost immediately into uncomfortable batting rather than the clean first-use rhythm they would have wanted. Zane Foster found the early front-pad line to remove Rowan Grant, Lucas Reed then got through Leo Bennett, and suddenly the innings had that thin, nervous shape where every dot ball sounded louder than it should. Isaac Ward and Connor Wells did the first necessary repair, and it was not glamorous cricket, more straight-bat survival and careful singles, but it stopped Gulfstone from turning two early wickets into a full opening collapse. Connor was the cleaner scorer in that passage, finding enough drives and clips to keep the board moving, while Isaac took the heavier patience role, letting the innings settle around him rather than trying to force it forward too early. When Connor Blake removed the captain and Caleb Marsh later had Isaac stumped, Summit Vale were still only partly rebuilt, four down and not yet in command. That was where Aaron Vale changed the match. Evan Reid gave him the kind of partner a new batter needs in that situation, quiet, careful, and present long enough to let the set-up breathe, and then Aaron began shifting the innings from repair into pressure. He did not start as a blind hitter. He waited for the lengths that belonged to him, then struck them with increasing violence, first through controlled drives, then through the pick-up shots that forced the field back, and eventually through the full range of clean hitting that made Gulfstone’s good start feel increasingly distant. The partnership with Evan Reid was the hinge because it carried Summit Vale out of the danger zone and into a position where the bowlers were no longer attacking a wounded innings; they were trying to contain a set batter who had taken control of the tempo. Evan fell after doing his job, and Luca Barrett gave Aaron a short supporting hand, but the innings’ next real push came with Liam Frost. Liam did not merely stand at the other end. He scored quickly enough to keep Gulfstone from hiding the field around Aaron, and that mattered because Aaron was now deep into the kind of innings that changes a captain’s plans every over. He eventually fell for a hundred that had carried the middle of the day, and although he did not bat through, the match had already been rewritten by then. Liam stayed unbeaten, Ryan Foster added a brief piece, and Mason Hart gave the last few balls a clean enough finish to push Summit Vale to 282 for eight. Gulfstone’s bowling had pieces to respect, especially the early accuracy of Zane Foster and Lucas Reed, Caleb Marsh’s control, and Connor Blake’s middle breakthrough, but they could not keep the match in the state they had created. The innings had moved from 23 for two to a score that demanded a serious chase, and the reason was Aaron Vale’s central hand, supported by enough late batting to make the total feel heavier than the first hour had promised. Gulfstone’s reply began with intent rather than panic. Owen Hayes played the sharper opening role, Ethan Ward gave the first stand a steadier shape, and for a few overs Summit Vale had to work rather than wait. Mason Hart broke that opening by bowling Owen, but Gulfstone were still alive while Ethan and Aiden Price tried to keep the chase orderly. Ryan Foster then produced the spell that changed the pursuit. He removed Aiden, then Caleb Marsh, and then Ethan Ward, and those wickets did more than decorate the scorecard; they broke the sequence of the chase. Gulfstone had gone from a working start to four down before the innings had fully settled, and from there they needed a real middle-order rebuild rather than one more brief counter. Noah Grant supplied the main resistance. He first worked with Daniel Cross, who gave the innings a useful burst before Luke Barrett removed him, and then Noah built the stand that made Summit Vale think hardest, this time with Zane Foster. Zane’s hand was important because it gave the chase a second current, boundaries at the right times, enough calm to keep the required rate from becoming imaginary, and enough partnership value to make Gulfstone’s dressing room believe there was still a route back. For a while, that sixth-wicket stand was the one passage where the chase felt like more than delayed defeat. Luke Barrett broke it by removing Zane, and Evan Turner followed by getting Noah soon after, which was the real end of Gulfstone’s argument. Connor Blake and Ryan Cole gave the final overs some movement, Lucas Reed came and went, Dylan Shaw lasted long enough to drag the innings a few balls deeper, but the chase had already lost its central pair and could not rebuild a second time. Summit Vale’s bowling was not perfect, yet it was timely: Mason Hart’s early control, Ryan Foster’s three-wicket damage, Luke Barrett’s two key breaks, Evan Turner’s removal of the set batter, and Liam Frost’s final wicket all arrived in the right places. Gulfstone were bowled out for 223, leaving Summit Vale winners by 59 runs. The match therefore turns on one clean cricketing truth: Gulfstone won the first hour, but Aaron Vale won the middle of the first innings, and that proved heavier than anything Gulfstone could assemble in response. Their chase had good fragments, especially Noah Grant and Zane Foster’s resistance, but Summit Vale owned the decisive passages, first through Aaron’s hundred, then through the wickets that kept cutting Gulfstone down just as the pursuit threatened to become real.
