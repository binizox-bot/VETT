Brookfield Oval in Eastmere had that hard brightness where the surface looked friendly from the boundary and less generous once the ball started landing on a length, so when Dune Rangers won the toss and asked Cedar Heights XI to bat first, the decision had enough logic behind it to feel sensible rather than defensive. For the opening spell it looked even better. Adrian Vale tried to seize the morning with a pair of sharp strokes, but Zane Foster drew him into one too many positive hands and the first wicket arrived before Cedar Heights had found any real rhythm. Nolan Price then spent a short, uncomfortable stay trying to survive Connor Blake’s straighter line before losing his stumps, and the early shape of the innings was suddenly awkward: movement on offer, fielders awake, the score moving in small pieces rather than in a clean flow. Mason Reid gave Owen Clarke brief company and played a couple of proper shots, yet he too fell before the innings had escaped the first danger zone, leaving Cedar Heights in the sort of position where a side batting first has to decide whether to keep playing the ideal innings or start building the innings actually available. Owen Clarke made that decision better than anyone else on the ground. At first he was watchful, almost deliberately quiet, letting the ball come, taking the single when it was safe and refusing the one that looked like a trap. Connor Shaw then joined him and became the exact partner Cedar Heights needed: steady, low-risk, hard to move, and patient enough to let the match stop feeling like a collapse. Their partnership did not begin as a storm. It began as repair work. The first job was to move the score out of that early damage, the second was to make Dune Rangers bowl longer spells without immediate reward, and only after that did the partnership begin to stretch into something that changed the entire game. Clarke was the clearer scoring force, gradually opening the off side, picking up anything too straight, and later using the bigger shot once the field finally had to retreat. Shaw’s value was less dramatic but just as important to the structure, because he kept the other end from becoming a pressure point and made Dune’s captain keep thinking about two batters rather than one. As the stand grew, the early wickets started to feel further away, and the fielding side’s small mistakes began to cost more: a wide here, a no-ball later, one misfield that turned a tight over into a slightly damaged one. Dylan Price eventually broke the stand by removing Shaw, and that dismissal at least stopped the innings from becoming completely unreachable, but by then Cedar Heights had moved from survival into command. Dylan Grant came in and gave the late overs a different temperature, hitting cleanly from the start and using the platform in exactly the way a fifth batter should. Clarke, already deep into a hundred, kept adding rather than simply protecting the milestone, and that is why the innings became heavy. Noah Grant did remove Dylan Grant and later Aiden Fox, Zane Foster returned to get Clarke near the end, and Ryan Cole’s run-out made the last few balls look messier on paper, but the damage had already been arranged. Cedar Heights closed on 314 for eight, a total built from early trouble, one huge fourth-wicket recovery, and enough late force to make the chase feel uphill before Dune Rangers had faced a ball. The first innings still gave Dune things to point at: Zane Foster had three wickets and real early impact, Connor Blake was the most controlled of the main bowlers, Caleb Brooks kept a lid on parts of the middle, and Noah Grant found two late strikes. The problem was that the central stand had bent the match out of its first shape. Chasing 315, Dune Rangers needed either a settled top order or one sustained counterattack, and they never quite found either for long enough. Liam Parker gave the start some life, Owen Hayes fell early to Ethan Parker, and when Aiden Price briefly flashed boundaries beside Noah Grant, the chase had its first little surge. Ethan Parker cut that off sharply, taking Aiden and then Evan Hart in the same early phase, which meant Dune were already four down before they had properly converted intent into stability. Noah Grant still threatened to turn the chase into something noisy. His innings was compact and dangerous, full of hard boundary options and enough clean hitting to make Cedar Heights spread the field earlier than they wanted. Caleb Brooks tried to steady the other end, and for a few overs Dune Rangers looked as if they might at least make the target feel alive. Zach Foster’s wicket of Noah was therefore a major break, not just another name in the book, because it removed the one batter who was scoring quickly enough to make the required rate feel negotiable. Caleb and Dylan Shaw then moved into a more careful rebuilding phase, but the rhythm of the chase had changed. Every quiet over now carried weight. Every single looked useful but not enough. Liam Reed entered as the bowler who turned that pressure into visible damage, first removing Caleb when the captain was trying to build a second platform, then pinning Dylan Shaw, and later bowling Lucas Reed before the lower middle could form a real bridge. At that point the chase was almost gone, yet Dylan Price gave Dune Rangers one last sharp, defiant passage. He hit with far more freedom than most of the innings had allowed, and Connor Blake stayed long enough for their stand to drag the score deep enough to make Cedar Heights work again rather than simply wait. That partnership did not threaten the result in a complete sense, but it did stop the match from ending as a simple procession. Connor Shaw eventually broke it by removing Connor Blake, and Ryan Cole closed the innings by bowling Zane Foster, leaving Dylan Price stranded and Dune Rangers all out for 227. The margin, 87 runs, suited the match’s actual shape. Dune Rangers had the early bowling burst, Noah Grant’s counterpunch, Caleb Brooks’ attempted middle repair and Dylan Price’s late resistance. Cedar Heights had the innings that mattered most, Owen Clarke’s 128, a score that did not merely decorate the card but transformed it after the early wickets. Ethan Parker’s new-ball and first-change damage, Liam Reed’s middle-lower strikes, Zach Foster’s removal of Noah, and the closing work from Connor Shaw and Ryan Cole then made sure the chase never became a full argument. Owen Clarke was the correct player of the match because he changed the game at its most vulnerable point, turning 55 for three into a commanding total, and once Cedar Heights had that scoreboard behind them, they defended with enough timing, control and patience to make every Dune Rangers recovery feel temporary.
